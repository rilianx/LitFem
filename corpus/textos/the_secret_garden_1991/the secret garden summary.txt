2. Comprehensive Plot Summary
A. Introduction

The narrative record commences in India, focusing on Mary Lennox, a nine-year-old girl described as "the most disagreeable-looking child ever seen." The text documents her physical appearance: "thin little face," "little thin body," "light thin hair," and a "sour expression." Her hair and face are yellow due to illness. Her father holds a position under the English Government and is perpetually busy and ill. Her mother is described as a "great beauty" who cares primarily for parties and gaiety. Mary has been kept out of sight, attended almost exclusively by native servants who obey her every command to keep her quiet, resulting in her becoming "as tyrannical and selfish a little pig as ever lived."

One morning, the routine is broken. Mary awakens to find her usual Ayah missing, replaced by a servant she dislikes. Noises and confusion fill the bungalow. Mary creates a flowerbed in the garden, sticking scarlet hibiscus blossoms into heaps of earth, muttering, "Pig! Pig! Daughter of Pigs!" regarding her absent Ayah. Later, she observes her mother on the veranda speaking with a young officer. Her mother appears frightened, stating, "Is it so very bad? Oh, is it?" The officer replies that people are dying "like flies." The cholera has broken out.

The household descends into chaos. Servants flee or fall ill. Mary hides in the nursery, forgotten. She sleeps and wakes to silence. The bungalow is deserted. She drinks a glass of wine left on a dining table and eats a biscuit. A snake glides under the door, watching her. She falls asleep again.

She is discovered by two officers entering the nursery. One exclaims, "Barney! There is a child here! A child alone! In a place like this! Mercy on us, who is she?" They identify her as "the child no one ever saw." Mary informs them, "I am Mary Lennox... I fell asleep when everyone had the cholera and I have only just wakened up." The men inform her that her parents and the Ayah are dead.

Mary is temporarily housed with an English clergyman, Mr. Crawford, and his family. The Crawford children torment her, calling her "Mistress Mary, quite contrary." Basil, one of the sons, informs her she is going to England to live with her uncle, Mr. Archibald Craven. He describes Mr. Craven: "He lives in a great, big, desolate old house in the country and no one goes near him... He's a hunchback, and he's horrid."

Mrs. Medlock, Mr. Craven's housekeeper, arrives to collect Mary. She is a "stout woman, with very red cheeks and sharp black eyes." They travel by ship and then by train to Yorkshire. During the journey, Mrs. Medlock provides Mary with details about her destination, Misselthwaite Manor. She states, "It's a grand big place in a gloomy way... The house is six hundred years old... It's on the edge of the moor." She adds regarding Mr. Craven: "He's got a crooked back... he was a sour young man and got no good of all his money and big place till he was married." She recounts that his wife died, leading to his current state: "He's queer now... He cares for nobody."

They arrive at the station at night and take a carriage. The drive is long. Mary looks out at the darkness. Mrs. Medlock identifies the landscape: "We're on the moor now." Mary perceives it as a "wide expanse of black ocean through which she was passing in and out of a strip of land." She asks, "Is it the sea?" Mrs. Medlock corrects her: "No, not it... It's just miles and miles and miles of wild land that nothing grows on but heather and gorse and broom, and nothing lives on but wild ponies and sheep."

The carriage arrives at a long, low house with stone pillars. The hall is immense and dimly lit. Mr. Pitcher, an elderly man, meets them, stating Mr. Craven does not wish to see them. Mary is led up a staircase, down a long corridor, and into a room with a fire. Mrs. Medlock announces: "This is your room... and you must keep to it. Don't you forget that!"
B. The Body (Rising Action)

Mary wakes the next morning to see a "wide stretch of land that seemed to have no trees on it, and to look rather like an endless, dull, purplish sea." Martha Sowerby, a young housemaid with a "round, rosy, good-natured face," attends to her. A conflict arises immediately regarding dressing. Mary expects to be dressed; Martha is confused, stating, "Canna' tha' dress thysen?" Mary, indignant, replies, "No... I never did in my life." Martha assists her but comments on her lack of autonomy.

Martha speaks in broad Yorkshire dialect, which Mary initially struggles to understand. Martha mentions her brother, Dickon, who "is twelve years old and he's got a young pony he calls his own." She describes Dickon wandering the moor for hours: "He says he can see what they're saying."

Mary explores the gardens. She encounters Ben Weatherstaff, an elderly gardener with a "surly old face." He is leaning on a spade. A robin redbreast appears. Ben speaks to the bird: "Here he is... the cheekiest little beggar in the world." Mary is fascinated. Ben explains the robin "hasn't got no wife," calling him a conceited bachelor. Ben observes Mary and comments, "Tha' an' me are a good bit alike... We're wove out of th' same cloth. We're neither of us good lookin' an' we're both of us as sour as we look."

Mary asks about the gardens. Ben lists the kitchen gardens and orchards but mentions one is closed. "There was one that Mr. Craven had shut up when his wife died... It's been shut up ten years." He notes the door is locked and the key buried.

Mary wanders the grounds and finds the ivy-covered walls. She hears a crying sound from within the house, "a curious sound... it was like a child crying." She asks Mrs. Medlock about it later. Mrs. Medlock looks frightened and denies it: "It's the wind... It's the wind wutherin' round the house."

Days pass. Mary spends more time outdoors, attempting to make a garden. Her health improves; she becomes less yellow and eats more. Martha brings her a skipping rope, a gift purchased with Martha's wages. Mary skips in the gardens, counting to one hundred.

While watching the robin in one of the walled gardens, the bird flies to a mound of freshly turned earth. Mary sees a brass ring. She digs and pulls out "a rusty old key." She realizes, "Perhaps it has been buried for ten years... Perhaps it is the key to the garden!"

The next day, the wind is strong. Mary returns to the ivy-covered wall. The robin chirps at her. A gust of wind swings aside a loose trail of ivy. Underneath, Mary sees "a round knob which had been covered by the leaves hanging over it." It is a door handle. She inserts the key. It turns. She pushes the door open and slips through.

She stands inside the secret garden. "It was the sweetest, most mysterious-looking place any one could imagine. The high walls which shut it in were covered with the leafless stems of climbing roses which were so thick that they were matted together." The ground is covered in brown grass and moss. She finds green shoots pushing through the earth. She begins to weed around them, using a piece of wood. She thinks, "I am the only person in the world who knows he is here."

Mary asks Martha for garden tools. Martha suggests Dickon can buy them. Mary writes a letter to Dickon. Martha sends it.

A few days later, Mary finds a boy sitting under a tree in the woods, playing a wooden pipe. It is Dickon. He is surrounded by a squirrel and a pheasant. He has "a curved, upturned nose and wide, open eyes" the color of the sky. He possesses a "clean, fresh scent of heather and grass and leaves." He brings the tools and flower seeds. Mary trusts him immediately. She takes him to the secret garden. Dickon looks round and whispers, "Eh! it is a queer, pretty place! It's like as if a body was in a dream." He examines the rose stems and cuts one, showing Mary the green inside: "It's wick... It's as wick as you or me." They work together, clearing the weeds. Mary swears him to secrecy.

That night, Mary hears the crying again. It is distinct. She leaves her room, traversing the dark corridors. She sees light under a door. She pushes it open and finds a large room with carved furniture. in a four-poster bed lies a boy "crying fretfully."

The boy sees her and gasps, "Who are you? ... Are you a ghost?" Mary replies, "No, I am not... I am Mary Lennox. Mr. Craven is my uncle." The boy identifies himself: "I am Colin."

Colin explains he is Mr. Craven's son. He has been ill since birth. "My father daren't let people see me... He is afraid I shall grow up to be like him." Colin believes he has a lump on his back and is going to die. He states, "I am going to die... My back is weak and my legs are all wrong." He spends his time in bed, reading and looking at pictures. He hates being looked at.

Mary informs him of the secret garden (without revealing she has entered it), describing what it might be like. Colin is intrigued. He asks her to visit him again. The next morning, Mary tells Martha she has found Colin. Martha is terrified she will lose her place. Mary asserts her dominance, stating she will do what she pleases.

Mary visits Colin regularly. They talk of the moor, Dickon, and the garden. Colin reveals his father visits him only when he is asleep because "I make him remember."

Mary and Dickon continue working in the garden. The spring advances; bulbs bloom—crocuses, snowdrops, daffodils. The grey world turns green. The robin builds a nest.

Colin undergoes a crisis. He has a tantrum, screaming hysterically. The nurse and Mrs. Medlock are helpless. Mary rushes to his room, shouting, "You stop! You stop! I hate you! Everybody hates you! I wish everybody would die!" The shock silences him. Colin sobs he can feel the lump on his back. Mary examines his back. She declares, "There's not a single lump there!... There's not a lump as big as a pin!" Colin is relieved. Mary stays with him until he sleeps, holding his hand and singing a Hindustani lullaby.

Mary brings Dickon to meet Colin. Dickon brings a lamb and his squirrel. Colin is mesmerized. He asks about the garden. They decide to take Colin out in his wheelchair.

They prepare for the outing. Colin gives orders that the gardeners must be kept away so no one looks at him. Dickon pushes the wheelchair. They enter the secret garden. Colin looks around. "The sun fell warm upon his face... He held himself up." He exclaims, "I shall get well! I shall get well!"

They spend the afternoon in the garden. While the sun sets, Ben Weatherstaff climbs a ladder on the other side of the wall to look over. He sees them. He is furious. "I never thought much of thee!" he shouts at Mary. Then he sees Colin. "Who is that?" he asks. " Cripple!"

Colin is enraged by the word "cripple." He commands, "Come here!" Ben descends the ladder and enters the garden. Colin, summoning all his strength, pushes himself up from the wheelchair. He stands upright, holding the arm of the chair only slightly. "Look at me!" he commands Ben. "Just you look at me!" He stands straight. Ben is awestruck. "Eh! the lies folks tells! tha'rt as thin as a lath an' as white as a wraith, but there's not a knob on thee."

Colin insists on standing and walking a few paces to prove his strength. He declares the garden is "Magic." He decides they will come every day. He will learn to walk. Ben is conscripted into the secret group.

The narrative documents the daily ritual. Colin, Mary, Dickon, and Ben sit in a circle under a tree. Colin chants, "The Magic is in me—the Magic is in me." He begins to walk, counting his steps. He exercises. He gains weight and color.

They must keep this a secret from the house staff to surprise Mr. Craven upon his return. Colin feigns illness and poor appetite indoors, while eating heartily of the food Dickon and Mary smuggle in (roasted potatoes, milk, oatcakes provided by Mrs. Sowerby).

Mrs. Sowerby (Dickon's mother) visits the garden. She brings a basket of food. She sees Colin. She exclaims, "Eh! dear lad! ... Tha'rt so like thy mother!" Colin asks if his father will like him. Mrs. Sowerby assures him, "He must love thee—he must."
C. Finale (Conclusion)

The focus shifts to Mr. Archibald Craven. He has been traveling through Europe—Switzerland, Italy, Norway—trying to escape his grief. In the Austrian Tyrol, he walks near a stream. He feels a sudden change. "He felt as if he were slowly coming back to life." He thinks, "I am almost well."

That night, he sleeps by a lake. He dreams. He hears a voice calling him: "Archie! Archie! Archie!" He answers, "Where are you?" The voice replies, "In the garden!" He wakes up. He receives a letter from Mrs. Sowerby the next morning. It reads: "I hope your Honor will pardon me... I think you would be glad to come home... I think you would be glad to see Master Colin."

Mr. Craven returns to Yorkshire immediately. He arrives at the Manor. He asks Mrs. Medlock about Colin. She reports, "He's very weird, sir... He's fatter... but he won't let us see him."

Mr. Craven goes out to the gardens. He heads toward the ivy-covered wall. He hears sounds—"running feet" and "laughing." He wonders, "Who is that?" He approaches the door.

Inside the garden, the children are running. Colin is shouting, "I can beat you! I can beat you!" He runs at full speed toward the door.

The door opens. Mr. Craven steps forward. Colin runs straight into him, almost knocking him over. Mr. Craven catches the boy. He looks at the boy's face. "He held him away to look at him and caught his breath."

"Who—what? Who is this?" Mr. Craven asks.

Colin stands straight. "Father," he says, "I'm Colin. You can't believe it. I scarcely can myself. I'm Colin."

Mr. Craven trembles. "In the garden," he says. "In the garden."

Colin explains: "It was the garden that did it—and Mary and Dickon and the creatures—and the Magic." He adds, "I'm well... I'm going to live to be a man."

Mr. Craven looks at the garden. "It is your mother's eyes I see... I see her eyes."

The group walks back to the house. Colin walks beside his father. "He looked as tall and straight as any boy of his age."

They approach the house. Mrs. Medlock and the staff are watching from the windows. Mrs. Medlock gasps. Mr. Pitcher clasps his hands.

The narrative concludes with the image of the return. "Across the lawn came the Master of Misselthwaite and he looked as he had not looked for ten years." Beside him walks a boy with his head up and his eyes full of laughter. The final sentence records the reaction of the observers as the father and son walk together: "Master Colin!"
