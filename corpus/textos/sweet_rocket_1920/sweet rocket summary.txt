2. Comprehensive Plot Summary (Written Order Only)
A. Introduction
The narrative begins with Marget Land in the city. The text presents her environment as urban, crowded, and physically taxing. Marget is positioned as a woman seeking departure. She navigates the heat and the noise of the streets. The text records her internal state as one of fatigue mixed with anticipation. She boards a train. The narrative details the physical sensation of the train moving away from the city center, passing through the industrial outskirts, and eventually entering the greener, open country.

Marget observes the changing landscape through the window. She reflects on her destination: Sweet Rocket. The text establishes that she has been hired as a secretary and companion. She does not know the full details of the employer but holds a letter of engagement.

Upon arrival at the station, she is met by a wagon. The drive to the estate is described with attention to the steepness of the hills and the density of the forest. The driver speaks little. Marget observes the transition from the public road to the private lane of Sweet Rocket. The narrative describes the house as an older structure, integrated into the landscape, surrounded by trees and overgrown gardens.

"The road went up and the road went down. It was a red road and rough, and the unkept bordering bushes whipped the wheels."

Marget arrives at the house. She enters the hall, which is described as cool and dim. She meets Mrs. Hypatia Lambert and Miss Darcy. They provide the initial welcome. The text records their greetings and their instructions regarding her room and her duties. Marget is shown to her room. She unpacks her belongings. The narrative focuses on her sensory experience of the room—the smell of lavender, the view from the window, the silence of the countryside compared to the city she left.

She descends to meet her employer, Richard Linden. The text reveals Richard Linden’s physical condition: he is blind. He stands by the hearth. Marget approaches him.

"He stood by the window, a tall, thin man, loosely built. He turned as she entered. He had the blind look, the listening look, the look of the veil and the wider vision."

Linden greets her. He speaks of the journey and the house. He asks if she is tired. Marget replies that she is not. Linden explains the nature of the work. He dictates letters and essays. He requires someone to read to him and to write for him. He states that Sweet Rocket is a place of quiet and work.

The text records their first conversation regarding the atmosphere of the house. Linden suggests that the house itself has a specific quality or "note." Marget agrees, stating she feels a difference here.

"There is a feeling here," said Marget. "It is like a pause in music." "It is a good place," said Linden. "It is a place where one may find oneself."

The narrative introduces the other inhabitants more fully during the first evening meal. Roger Penryn and Curtin are present. The conversation at the table covers the logistics of the estate and general philosophical inquiries. Curtin is presented as a man of practical concerns, while Penryn appears more aligned with Linden’s abstract interests.

The first section concludes with Marget retiring for the night. She looks out at the woods surrounding Sweet Rocket. She feels a sense of detachment from her past life in the city. The narrative records her thought that she has entered a distinct sphere of existence.

"The night was a dark blue cup. The stars were grains of gold dust in the wine."

B. The Body (Rising Action)
The narrative progresses through a series of days, marked less by dramatic external events and more by the evolution of dialogue and internal perception. The text records Marget’s integration into the routine of Sweet Rocket.

Marget begins her work with Linden. They sit in the library or the garden. Linden dictates correspondence. The text presents the content of these dictations as philosophical treatises concerning the nature of reality, the human consciousness, and the concept of "dimensions." Marget writes down his words. She occasionally interrupts to ask for clarification. Linden responds with analogies involving maps, roads, and spatial relationships.

"We are creating," dictated Linden, "the new world. It is not a discovery of a new continent on the old globe. It is the seeing of the globe itself with new eyes."

The narrative follows Marget on her solitary walks through the woods of Sweet Rocket. She observes the flora—the ferns, the hemlocks, the mountain laurel. The text describes these walks in minute detail. Marget perceives the forest not as static scenery but as a living, vibrating entity. She stops to touch the bark of trees. She listens to the water in the creek.

"The brook ran over the stones. It said, 'Here! Here! Here!' It never ceased saying 'Here!'"

During one such walk, she encounters Roger Penryn. They walk together. Penryn speaks of his own reasons for being at Sweet Rocket. He describes a desire to escape the fragmentation of the modern world. He speaks of Linden’s influence. Penryn states that Linden sees more without eyes than most men do with them.

"He lives," said Penryn, "in a larger room. We are mostly in the cupboards. He is out in the hall."

The narrative shifts to a group gathering on the porch in the evening. Linden, Mrs. Lambert, Miss Darcy, Curtin, Penryn, and Marget are present. The conversation turns to the concept of time. Linden argues that the past, present, and future are constructs of a limited consciousness. He posits that in a higher state of awareness, these divisions dissolve. Curtin challenges this, citing the practical necessity of time. Linden counters with the metaphor of the traveler on a hill who sees the road behind and the road ahead simultaneously.

"If you are the road," said Linden, "you are all of it at once. If you are the traveler, you take it step by step."

Marget listens. The text records her internal processing of these ideas. She begins to experience moments where her perception shifts. The narrative describes these moments as "widenings." She looks at a flower and feels she is the flower. She looks at Linden and feels she understands his blindness not as darkness, but as a different form of light.

Days pass. The text documents a specific excursion where the group goes further into the hills. They bring a picnic. The description focuses on the physical exertion of the climb and the expansive views from the ridge. They sit on the rocks.

Linden speaks of the "Great Consciousness." He describes humanity as waking up from a sleep. He states that the individual self is a shell that must be broken to reveal the universal self.

"The shell breaks," said Linden. "The bird comes forth. It does not go back into the shell."

Marget asks if this process is painful. Linden replies that the breaking of the shell is struggle, but the flight is joy.

The narrative introduces a conflict involving a neighbor, a local farmer who disputes a boundary line. Curtin is agitated by this. He discusses legal recourse and fences. Linden listens quietly. He advises patience. He suggests that the boundary is less real than the neighbor’s goodwill. He instructs Curtin to speak with the man again, not about the land, but about the man’s family and needs. The text records Curtin’s skepticism but his eventual agreement to try Linden’s method.

Later, Marget reads to Linden from a book of poetry. Linden interrupts to comment on a line. He speaks of the "Sweet Rocket" metaphor—the flower that grows wild and sweet. He compares the estate to the flower, a place that grows naturally without forced cultivation. He tells Marget that she is like the Sweet Rocket, hardy and fragrant.

"You have the earth strength," said Linden. "And you have the air turning."

The text records a shift in the weather. A storm approaches. The sky darkens. The wind increases. The group gathers inside. The atmosphere in the house becomes charged. They sit by the fire. The conversation turns to fear. Miss Darcy expresses a fear of the storm. Linden speaks of fear as a contraction of the spirit. He suggests that if one expands to include the storm, there is no fear.

"Be the storm," said Linden. "Do not be the little thing the storm beats upon."

Marget practices this. She closes her eyes. The narrative describes her internal effort to expand her sense of self to encompass the wind and the thunder. She reports a sensation of peace amidst the noise.

Subsequent chapters focus on individual interactions between Marget and the other guests. Marget and Mrs. Lambert discuss the history of the house. Mrs. Lambert tells stories of the people who lived there before. She speaks of the accumulation of thoughts and feelings in the walls. She suggests that the house remembers.

Marget and Curtin discuss Linden. Curtin expresses loyalty but also confusion. He admits he does not always understand Linden’s theories but he feels the man’s goodness. He recounts how Linden helped him when he was destitute.

The narrative returns to Marget’s work. She is typing a manuscript for Linden. The text provides excerpts of the manuscript. It deals with the "Fourth Dimension" as a dimension of consciousness, not just geometry. It speaks of "Cosmic Consciousness."

"We are moving," wrote Marget, typing the words, "into the region of the whole. The part ceases to claim the whole. The whole claims the part."

One afternoon, a stranger arrives at Sweet Rocket. He is a traveler who has lost his way. He is invited in. The narrative records his reaction to the group. He is puzzled by their conversation but drawn to the atmosphere. He stays for dinner. He asks Linden about his blindness. Linden laughs and says he sees quite well. The stranger leaves the next day, stating he feels refreshed.

The narrative documents a deepening of the relationship between Marget and Linden. It is not presented as a traditional romance but as a "recognition." They sit together in silence for long periods. The text describes a communication that happens without words. Marget feels she knows what Linden is thinking. Linden answers her unspoken questions.

"You asked," said Linden, breaking the silence, "if death is the end. It is the end of the limitation, not the end of the music." "I did not speak," said Marget. "You spoke loudly," said Linden.

The seasons begin to change. The text notes the turning of the leaves. The air grows colder. The group’s discussions turn to the future. They discuss the possibility of leaving Sweet Rocket. Marget feels a pang of sorrow at the thought of returning to the city. Linden assures her that Sweet Rocket is a state of mind, not just a place. He tells her she carries it with her.

There is a sequence where Marget visits the village near the estate. She observes the lives of the villagers. She sees poverty and struggle. She contrasts this with the peace of Sweet Rocket. She questions if it is right to stay in the retreat while the world suffers. She brings this question to Linden.

Linden responds that one cannot help the world by drowning with it. He states that by holding the light, one helps those in the dark. He argues that the work done at Sweet Rocket—the work of generating peace and higher thought—radiates outward.

"The lighthouse does not run about the sea," said Linden. "It stands and shines."

The body of the narrative continues with a series of "realizations" for Marget. She realizes that her previous life was a form of sleep. She realizes that the separation between herself and others is an illusion. She experiences a moment in the garden where time seems to stop. The text describes the sunlight, the bees, and the movement of the leaves as a single, unified event.

"The moment opened," the text records. "It was not a second ticking. It was a door swinging wide."

Curtin returns from the neighbor. He reports that the dispute is settled. The neighbor was disarmed by the lack of aggression. They shook hands. Curtin is amazed. He tells Linden that the method worked. Linden nods.

"Force meets force," said Linden. "Love meets need."

The section nears its end with a collective experience. The group is gathered. They are reading aloud. A feeling of intense unity descends upon them. The text describes it as a "presence." No one speaks. They sit in the presence. The narrative states that they felt the walls of the room dissolve and the stars enter.

C. Finale (Conclusion)
The narrative moves to the final phase of Marget’s stay, or rather, the establishment of a permanent state of being.

The text describes the onset of autumn fully. The woods are red and gold. The air is crisp. Marget and Linden walk to the gate of Sweet Rocket.

They discuss the inevitable return to the world for some, and the staying for others. Marget explicitly asks if she must leave. Linden asks her what she desires.

"I desire to be where the work is," said Marget. "The work is everywhere," said Linden. "But the center is here for you now."

It is established that Marget will stay at Sweet Rocket for the time being. She accepts her role not just as a secretary, but as a partner in the "creation."

The narrative records a final gathering. It is a summing up of the summer’s experiences. Each character speaks of what they have found.

Mrs. Lambert speaks of peace. Miss Darcy speaks of courage. Roger Penryn speaks of clarity. Curtin speaks of a new way of dealing with men.

Linden speaks last. He speaks of the "Kingdom." He states that the Kingdom is here, present, waiting only to be recognized. He raises his hand.

"It is nearer than hands and feet," said Linden. "It is closer than breathing."

The text describes the final morning recorded in the book. Marget wakes early. She goes out into the dew. The sun is rising. She looks back at the house, Sweet Rocket. She sees it as a vessel of light.

She encounters Linden in the garden. He is facing the sun. He cannot see the physical sun, but the text states he is drinking in the warmth and the "inner sun."

Marget stands beside him. They do not speak immediately. Then Linden turns to her.

"The day begins," said Linden. "The day is always beginning," said Marget.

The narrative concludes with an affirmation of continuity. There is no dramatic closure, no death, no departure. There is an assertion of a state of consciousness that has been achieved and will be maintained. The text leaves the characters in the garden, in the light, fully aware of the "Great Consciousness" they have discussed throughout the book.

The final lines reiterate the theme of the "unseen" being more real than the seen. Marget looks at the hills. She sees the physical hills, but she also sees the "hills of the spirit."

"The visible is the shoreline," the text concludes. "The invisible is the sea."

The narrative ends on this image of the sea and the shore, the finite and the infinite touching at Sweet Rocket.
