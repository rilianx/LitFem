Comprehensive Plot Summary
A. Introduction

The Invocation and the Plague The narrative begins with an invocation to the Muse to sing of the "anger of Peleus' son Achilleus / and its devastation." The text immediately situates the reader in the ninth year of the war between the Achaeans (Greeks) and the Trojans.

The conflict initiates not on the battlefield, but within the Achaean camp. Chryses, a priest of Apollo, arrives at the Achaean ships carrying a golden staff and fillets. He petitions the Atreidae (Agamemnon and Menelaus) and the other Achaeans to release his daughter, Chryseis, who has been taken as a war prize. While the army shouts their assent to respect the priest and take the ransom, Agamemnon rejects the plea violently.

    "Old man, do not let me find you loitering by the hollow ships / ... The girl I will not give back; sooner will old age come upon her / in my own house, in Argos, far from her own land."

Chryses departs in silence along the beach and prays to Apollo for retribution. Apollo hears him and descends from Olympus, "anger in his heart," carrying his silver bow. For nine days, the god rains arrows upon the camp, killing mules, dogs, and men.

The Council and the Quarrel On the tenth day, Achilles, prompted by the goddess Hera, calls an assembly. He suggests consulting a prophet to understand Apollo's rage. Calchas, the seer, rises but demands protection from Achilles before speaking, fearing the wrath of a king. Achilles guarantees his safety. Calchas reveals that Apollo is punishing them for Agamemnon’s dishonor of Chryses.

Agamemnon rises, "his heart filled with black anger." He calls Calchas a "prophet of evil" but agrees to return the girl to save the army, on one condition: he must receive a new prize to replace her, lest he be the only Argive without one.

Achilles objects, noting that all plunder has already been distributed. He proposes that Agamemnon return the girl now, and the Achaeans will repay him three or four times over when Troy falls. Agamemnon refuses, threatening to seize a prize from Achilles, Ajax, or Odysseus personally.

Achilles responds with immediate vitriol, stating he has no quarrel with the Trojans himself but fights only for Agamemnon's sake. He threatens to return home to Phthia. Agamemnon dismisses him, stating he does not need him, and declares his intent to take Briseis, Achilles' war prize.

    "I shall take the fair-cheeked Briseis, / your prize, I myself going to your shelter, that you may learn well / how much greater I am than you."

Achilles contemplates killing Agamemnon, drawing his sword halfway. Athena descends, visible only to Achilles, sent by Hera. She seizes him by his golden hair and commands him to cease the violence, promising him "three times such shining gifts" in the future for this outrage. Achilles obeys and sheathes his sword but verbally lashes Agamemnon, swearing a mighty oath on a scepter that a time will come when the Achaeans will yearn for him while dying under Hector’s hands.

    "And this shall be a great oath before you: / ... someday longing for Achilleus will come to the sons of the Achaeans, / all of them."

The Withdrawal The assembly dissolves. Odysseus leads a mission to return Chryseis to her father. Agamemnon dispatches his heralds, Talthybios and Eurybates, to Achilles' tent to seize Briseis. They find Achilles sitting by his ships. He surrenders the woman without resistance to the heralds but commands Patroclus to bring her out, reiterating his withdrawal from the war.

Achilles sits alone by the surf and weeps, calling to his mother, Thetis. She emerges from the sea. He recounts the dishonor and asks her to petition Zeus: to aid the Trojans and pin the Achaeans against their ships, so Agamemnon may realize his "madness."

Intervention on Olympus Thetis ascends to Olympus and clasps Zeus’ knees, begging him to honor her son. Zeus sits in silence for a long time but eventually agrees, despite fearing Hera’s reaction. He nods his head, shaking the great Olympus, confirming the promise.

Hera sees Thetis departing and accuses Zeus of plotting. Zeus warns her to sit down and be silent, threatening her with violence. Hephaestus intervenes to diffuse the tension, serving nectar to the gods. The divine scene ends with feasting and the gods retiring to sleep.
B. The Body (Rising Action)

The False Dream and the Catalog Zeus sends a Dream (Oneiros) to Agamemnon, instructing him to arm the Achaeans, falsely promising that he will capture Troy that day. Agamemnon wakes, believes the dream, and calls a council. To test the morale of the troops, he deceptively proposes that they should flee to their ships and return home. The test fails; the soldiers, exhausted by nine years of war, rush joyfully to the ships to launch them.

Hera dispatches Athena to stop the rout. Athena finds Odysseus, who casts off his cloak and takes Agamemnon’s scepter. He moves through the crowd, physically striking commoners and persuading leaders to return to the assembly. He silences Thersites, a common soldier who abuses Agamemnon, by beating him with the scepter. The army regroups.

Nestor advises Agamemnon to arrange the troops by tribe and clan. The narrative then provides the Catalog of Ships, listing the leaders and contingents of the Achaean forces, followed by the Trojan battle order.

The Duel for Helen The armies advance. Paris (Alexandros) steps forward and challenges the best Achaeans to single combat. When Menelaus steps out to fight him, Paris retreats into the ranks, "shaken to his heart." Hector rebukes Paris for his cowardice and beauty, wishing he had never been born.

    "Evil Paris, beautiful, woman-crazy, cajoling, / better had you never been born or killed unwedded."

Paris agrees to a duel with Menelaus to decide the war: the winner takes Helen and the treasure, and the rest swear oaths of friendship. Iris visits Helen, who is weaving a tapestry of the war's struggles, and urges her to the Scaean Gates. There, Priam asks Helen to identify the Greek heroes visible from the wall (The Teichoskopia). She identifies Agamemnon, Odysseus, Ajax, and Idomeneus.

The duel commences. Menelaus shatters his sword on Paris's helmet, then drags Paris by the helmet strap. Aphrodite intervenes, snapping the strap and whisking Paris away in a mist, depositing him in his bedroom. She forces Helen to go to him. Helen insults Paris but eventually lies down with him. On the battlefield, Menelaus searches for Paris, and Agamemnon declares Menelaus the victor, demanding the return of Helen.

The Breaking of Oaths On Olympus, Zeus suggests ending the war since Menelaus won, but Hera and Athena demand the destruction of Troy. Zeus relents and sends Athena to trigger a violation of the truce. Athena disguises herself and persuades the archer Pandarus to shoot an arrow at Menelaus. The arrow strikes Menelaus but only wounds him. The truce is broken, and battle erupts.

The Aristeia of Diomedes Diomedes dominates the battlefield, inspired by Athena. He kills Pandarus and wounds Aeneas. When Aphrodite attempts to rescue her son Aeneas, Diomedes pursues the goddess and stabs her in the wrist. She flees to Olympus, sobbing. Diomedes then attacks Apollo, who is guarding Aeneas. Apollo rebukes him:

    "Take care, give back, son of Tydeus, and strive no longer / to make yourself like the gods in mind."

Diomedes withdraws. Ares enters the battle on the Trojan side. Athena joins Diomedes in his chariot, and together they drive a spear into Ares' belly. Ares screams and flees to Olympus.

Hector and Andromache Hector returns to Troy to ask the women to pray to Athena. He visits his home and finds his wife, Andromache, on the wall with their infant son, Astyanax. Andromache begs him not to return to the fight, predicting his death and her enslavement.

    "Dearest, / your own great strength will be your death, and you have no pity / on your little son, nor on me, ill-starred."

Hector acknowledges her fear but refuses to withdraw, stating he would feel deep shame to shrink from battle. He reaches for his son, who cries at the sight of the horsehair crest on his helmet. Hector removes the helmet, holds the boy, and prays he becomes a greater man than his father. He returns to battle with Paris.

The Duel of Hector and Ajax Hector challenges a Greek champion to single combat. The Achaeans cast lots, and Ajax the Great is chosen. They fight until nightfall without a victor. They exchange gifts—Hector gives a sword, Ajax a purple war belt—and part as friends.

The Embassy to Achilles The Trojans begin to drive the Achaeans back. Agamemnon, weeping, admits his error and proposes giving up. Diomedes rejects this. Nestor advises Agamemnon to make amends with Achilles. Agamemnon agrees, offering massive gifts and the return of Briseis (whom he claims he never touched).

Odysseus, Ajax, and Phoenix go to Achilles' tent. They find him playing a lyre. Odysseus presents the offer eloquently, emphasizing the desperate state of the army. Achilles rejects it outright, delivering a long speech on the inevitability of death and his hatred for Agamemnon.

    "For not / worth the value of my life are all the possessions they fable / were won for Ilion ... / A man's life cannot come back again, it cannot be lifted / nor captured again by force, once it has crossed the teeth's barrier."

Phoenix, Achilles' tutor, tells the story of Meleager to warn him against waiting too long to help, but Achilles remains unmoved. He tells them he may sail home tomorrow. The embassy returns in failure.

The Night Raid Diomedes and Odysseus volunteer for a night reconnaissance mission. They capture a Trojan spy, Dolon, who reveals the location of the allies. They kill Dolon, then infiltrate the Thracian camp, killing King Rhesus and stealing his prized horses.

The Battle at the Ships The next morning, Agamemnon leads a charge but is wounded, as are Diomedes and Odysseus. The Achaeans are pushed back to their defensive wall. Hector, ignoring an omen of an eagle dropping a snake, leads the assault on the fortifications. Sarpedon pulls down part of the battlement. Hector smashes the gates with a massive stone, and the Trojans pour in, threatening to burn the ships.

Poseidon, seeing the Achaeans suffering, disobeys Zeus and inspires the Achaeans. Hera distracts Zeus by seducing him. She borrows Aphrodite's girdle and bribes Sleep. Zeus and Hera make love on Mount Ida, and a golden cloud covers them. While Zeus sleeps, Poseidon openly leads the Achaean counterattack. Hector is struck by a rock from Ajax and vomits blood, carried off the field.

Zeus Awakens Zeus wakes, sees the Trojans routed, and threatens Hera. He sends Apollo to heal Hector and inspire the Trojans. Hector returns, and the Achaeans panic. The Trojans push the Greeks back to the ships again. Hector grabs the stern of a ship and calls for fire.

Patroclus Intervenes Patroclus goes to Achilles, weeping. He criticizes Achilles' bitterness but asks permission to wear Achilles' armor and lead the Myrmidons into battle to save the ships.

    "Give me your armour to wear on my shoulders into the fighting; / so the Trojans might suppose I am you and hold back from their attack."

Achilles agrees but gives a strict command: Patroclus must drive the Trojans from the ships but must not pursue them to Troy, lest he rob Achilles of his glory or face a god's wrath.

Patroclus dons the armor (except the spear, which only Achilles can wield). He leads the Myrmidons as the first ship is set on fire. The Trojans, seeing the armor, believe Achilles has returned and flee. Patroclus kills Sarpedon, Zeus's son. Zeus rains blood in grief. Patroclus, intoxicated by success, ignores Achilles' order and chases the Trojans to the walls of the city.

The Death of Patroclus Apollo strikes Patroclus across the back, knocking off his helmet and armor. Euphorbus wounds him with a spear. Hector finishes him with a spear thrust to the belly. As Patroclus dies, he prophesies Hector’s death.

    "You yourself are not one who shall live long, but now already / death and powerful destiny are standing beside you, / to go down under the hands of Aiakos' great son, Achilleus."

Hector takes Achilles' armor from Patroclus’s body and puts it on. A fierce battle erupts over Patroclus’s corpse. Menelaus and Ajax defend it.

The Message Antilochus brings the news to Achilles. Achilles collapses in grief, clawing the ground and pouring dust over his head. His "terrible, wrenching cry" is heard by Thetis. She comes to him. He accepts that he must kill Hector, knowing his own death will follow shortly after.

Achilles walks to the trench, unaccompanied and unarmed. Athena hangs the aegis shield from his shoulders and crowns his head with a golden cloud of fire. He shouts three times. The sound alone throws the Trojan army into chaos, allowing the Achaeans to retrieve Patroclus’s body.

The Shield of Achilles Thetis visits Hephaestus to ask for new armor. Hephaestus creates a magnificent shield, decorating it with images of the cosmos, two cities (one at peace, one at war), ploughing, harvesting, dancing, and the Ocean river.

The Reconciliation and the Slaughter Achilles receives the armor. He calls an assembly and formally ends his anger against Agamemnon. Agamemnon blames "Delusion" (Ate) for his actions and returns the gifts and Briseis. Achilles is indifferent to the gifts and refuses to eat, wishing only for blood.

Achilles mounts his chariot. His horse, Xanthus, speaks, prophesying his death. Achilles drives into battle. He slaughters Trojans indiscriminately, clogging the river Xanthus (Scamander) with corpses. The river god rises in anger, complaining that his waters cannot flow. He attacks Achilles with waves. Achilles is nearly drowned until Hephaestus uses fire to boil the river, forcing it to submit.

The Gods at War Zeus permits the gods to intervene openly. Athena knocks down Ares. Poseidon challenges Apollo, but Apollo refuses to fight his uncle over mortals.

The Final Pursuit Priam opens the gates to let the fleeing Trojans in, then closes them. Hector remains alone outside the Scaean Gates, ignoring the pleas of Priam and Hecuba to enter. He debates retreating but fears the scorn of Polydamas and the citizens. He resolves to fight.

Achilles approaches. Hector's nerve fails, and he flees. Achilles chases him three times around the walls of Troy. Zeus weighs their fates in his golden scales; Hector's side sinks toward Hades. Apollo abandons Hector.
C. Finale (Conclusion)

The Death of Hector Athena disguises herself as Deiphobus, Hector’s brother, and appears beside him, pledging assistance. Encouraged, Hector stops and faces Achilles. He attempts to negotiate that the victor shall return the loser's body, but Achilles refuses.

    "There are no trustworthy oaths between men and lions, / nor wolves and lambs have spirit that can be brought to agreement."

Achilles throws his spear; Hector dodges. Athena returns the spear to Achilles unseen by Hector. Hector throws his spear, hitting Achilles' shield but bouncing off. He calls to Deiphobus for a lance, but no one is there. Hector realizes the gods have summoned him to death. He draws his sword and charges. Achilles targets the gap in the armor at the throat—the armor that was once his own—and drives the spear through Hector’s neck.

Hector falls but can still speak. He begs Achilles to ransom his body. Achilles replies:

    "No more entreating of me, you dog, by knees or parents. / I wish only that my spirit and fury would drive me / to hack your meat away and eat it raw for the things that you have done to me."

Hector dies, prophesying Achilles' death at the Scaean Gates by Paris and Apollo. Achilles strips the armor and the other Achaeans run up to stab the corpse. Achilles pierces Hector’s ankles, threads a leather strap through them, ties him to the chariot, and drags him through the dust back to the ships. Priam and Hecuba wail from the walls; Andromache collapses upon seeing the body dragged.

The Funeral Games Achilles continues to mourn Patroclus, refusing to wash the blood from his body. Patroclus’s ghost appears to him in a dream, demanding burial so he can enter Hades.

    "You sleep, Achilleus; you have forgotten me; but you were not / careless of me when I lived, but only in death ... / Bury me as quickly as may be, let me pass through the gates of Hades."

Achilles holds a massive funeral pyre. He cuts his hair and sacrifices twelve Trojan youths, dogs, and horses on the pyre. The winds Boreas and Zephyrus are summoned to fan the flames.

Following the cremation, Achilles hosts funeral games: chariot racing, boxing, wrestling, foot racing, and spear throwing. He distributes prizes generously, resolving disputes and acting as a composed host, contrasting with his earlier rage.

The Abuse of the Body The games end, but Achilles cannot sleep. For twelve days, he drags Hector’s body around Patroclus’s tomb every morning. Apollo protects the corpse from decay and disfigurement. The gods debate. Hera, Poseidon, and Athena oppose returning the body, but Zeus decrees that Achilles must accept a ransom. He sends Thetis to instruct Achilles, and Iris to instruct Priam.

The Ransom (Priam's Journey) Priam prepares a wagon with immense treasure. Hecuba tries to stop him, fearing he will be killed, but he insists. Hermes, disguised as a young Myrmidon, guides Priam safely through the Achaean lines to Achilles’ lodge.

Priam enters unnoticed, kneels, clasps Achilles' knees, and kisses the hands that killed his sons.

    "I have gone through what no other mortal on earth has gone through; / I put my lips to the hands of the man who killed my children."

Priam asks Achilles to remember his own father, Peleus. Achilles is moved to tears, thinking of his father and Patroclus. He gently pushes the old man away, and they weep together—Priam for Hector, Achilles for Peleus and Patroclus.

Achilles agrees to return the body. He orders the body washed and anointed secretly so Priam does not see the damage and provoke a rage in Achilles that might lead him to kill the old man. Achilles invites Priam to eat, citing the story of Niobe. They share a meal and admire each other's physical presence.

Achilles grants a truce of eleven days for Hector's funeral. Priam sleeps in the porch but is woken by Hermes and leaves before dawn with the body.

The Burial of Hector Priam returns to Troy with the body. Cassandra spots them first. The women of Troy—Andromache, Hecuba, and Helen—mourn over the body. Helen speaks last, noting that in twenty years, Hector never spoke a harsh word to her.

    "But if any other spoke harshly to me in the palace ... / you would speak and put them off and hold them back by your gentleness / and your gentle words."

For nine days, the Trojans gather wood. On the tenth day, they burn Hector on the pyre. They quench the embers with wine, gather his white bones in a golden urn, and bury them in a grave, piling stones over it. Sentries watch to ensure the Achaeans do not attack.

The narrative concludes with the completion of the burial rites.

    "Such was their burial of Hector, breaker of horses."
