Narrator Metadata

Narrator Voice: Satirical, Omniscient, Performative "Manager of the Performance"
Narrator Perspective: Third-person omniscient with first-person interruptions
Narrator Type: Intrusive / Puppet Master
Knowledge Scope: Universal, including private thoughts and historical context
Narrative Mediation: High; frequent direct address to the reader and meta-fictional commentary

2. Comprehensive Plot Summary
A. Introduction

The narrative opens at Miss Pinkerton’s academy for young ladies on Chiswick Mall. The "great iron gate" closes for the final time on Amelia Sedley and Rebecca Sharp. Miss Pinkerton oversees the departure, dictating a letter of recommendation for Amelia, whom she considers a model pupil, but refuses to do the same for Rebecca. Rebecca, an articled pupil and the daughter of an impoverished artist and a French opera dancer, is leaving to take a position as a governess. As they depart in the carriage, Miss Pinkerton offers a copy of Johnson’s Dictionary to Amelia, which she accepts gratefully. A second copy is offered to Rebecca.

    "Becky took it as kindly as it was meant... but the moment the coach drove off... Miss Sharp put her pale face out of the window and actually flung the book back into the garden."

Rebecca exclaims, "So much for the Dictionary; and, thank God, I'm out of Chiswick."

The two young women arrive at the Sedley home in Russell Square. Here, the contrast between their circumstances is established. Amelia is surrounded by a wealthy, doting family. Rebecca is an orphan with no fortune, dependent on her wit. Rebecca immediately sets her sights on Amelia's brother, Joseph "Jos" Sedley, a collector of Boggley Wollah in the East India Company service. Jos is described as a vain, shy, and obese man who loves food and clothing.

Rebecca employs flattery to engage Jos. During a dinner, Jos encourages Rebecca to try an Indian dish.

    "It is Cayenne pepper," said Joseph... "How they sting!" said he, wiping his mouth... "I should like to taste it," said Miss Adair... "It's capital," said he. "By Jove, it's the best thing I ever tasted."

Rebecca attempts the curry but is overwhelmed by the heat. "Water, for Heaven's sake, water!" she cries, yet she manages to turn the incident into a moment of shared intimacy with Jos, claiming she will learn to like it to please him.

The group, including George Osborne (Amelia’s betrothed) and his friend Captain William Dobbin, visits Vauxhall Gardens. Dobbin, tall, awkward, and secretly in love with Amelia, buys tickets and arranges the outing but is ignored. George Osborne is attentive to Amelia but arrogant. At the gardens, Jos drinks a significant amount of rack punch. Under the influence of alcohol, he becomes boisterous and nearly proposes to Rebecca, calling her his "did-dle-iddle-darling." However, the group is separated, and no proposal occurs.

The following morning, George Osborne visits Jos, who is suffering from a hangover. George mocks Jos's behavior from the previous night, convincing him that he made a fool of himself. Humiliated, Jos writes a note of apology to the ladies and flees to Leamington, abandoning any potential courtship with Rebecca.

Rebecca, her first scheme failed, must now depart for her post as governess. She leaves the Sedley home to travel to Queen’s Crawley.

    "I shall write to you, dear," she said to Amelia... "and we shall meet often, I hope... and I shall always be your friend."

B. The Body (Rising Action)

Rebecca arrives at Queen’s Crawley, the estate of the coarse and miserly baronet, Sir Pitt Crawley. She expects a noble mansion but finds a dilapidated house. Sir Pitt is described not as a refined gentleman, but as a man in "draggled gaiters and a dirty old coat." He speaks in a rustic dialect and engages in litigation with his neighbors.

Rebecca takes charge of her pupils, the younger daughters of Sir Pitt, and ingratiates herself with the entire household. She plays backgammon with Sir Pitt, helps him with his accounts, and listens to his endless stories about lawsuits. She writes to Amelia, describing the "sheep-washing, the pig-killing, the number of cramp-rings" she encounters.

The narrative introduces the rest of the Crawley family: Sir Pitt’s downtrodden second wife, Rose; his eldest son, Mr. Pitt Crawley, a pious and diplomatic man; and his younger son, Rawdon Crawley, a dragoon officer described as a heavy gambler and sportsman.

Events shift to the visit of Miss Matilda Crawley, Sir Pitt’s wealthy spinster half-sister. She possesses seventy thousand pounds, making her the object of the family's sycophancy. Miss Crawley despises her brother’s coarseness and the eldest son’s piety but takes a liking to Rawdon and, significantly, to Rebecca. She calls Rebecca a "little piquante thing" and values her wit and sarcasm.

When Miss Crawley becomes ill, she demands Rebecca accompany her back to her London home in Park Lane. Rebecca leaves Queen’s Crawley, effectively becoming Miss Crawley’s companion.

Back in the Sedley household, rumors of war circulate, but a more immediate financial disaster strikes. John Sedley, Amelia’s father, is rumored to be speculating heavily.

At Park Lane, Rebecca solidifies her position. Sir Pitt Crawley’s wife dies. Almost immediately, Sir Pitt travels to London and proposes marriage to Rebecca.

    "I want you back at Queen's Crawley, miss," the Baronet said... "I'll make you a zettlement. I'll do everything reglar... Say yes, Becky... I'm an old man, but a good'n... Come and be my wife."

Rebecca weeps and reveals a shocking truth: "Oh, Sir Pitt... Oh, sir—I—I'm married already."

It is revealed that Rebecca has secretly married Rawdon Crawley. Miss Crawley is furious, not because of the class difference alone, but because Rawdon has married a "nobody." She banishes them both. Rawdon and Rebecca retreat to lowly lodgings, assuming Miss Crawley will eventually forgive her favorite nephew. She does not.

Simultaneously, the financial ruin of John Sedley becomes a fact. The family is destitute. Mr. John Osborne, George’s father, a wealthy merchant who rose with Sedley’s help, now brutally cuts ties. He forbids George from marrying Amelia.

    "I wash my hands of the whole business," old Osborne said... "You may marry her if you like, but I'll cut you off with a shilling."

George is vain and selfish, but he is urged toward honor by William Dobbin. Dobbin, knowing Amelia will die of a broken heart without George, locates George and convinces him to defy his father. Dobbin arranges the wedding and pays for the license and the breakfast. George marries Amelia.

Old Osborne disinherits George, striking his name from the family Bible.

    "He drew a pen through the name of George Osborne... and wrote 'Dead' against the date of his marriage."

The military orders arrive: Napoleon has returned from Elba. The regiment, including George, Dobbin, and Rawdon, is ordered to Belgium. The couples—George and Amelia, Rawdon and Rebecca—travel to Brussels.

In Brussels, the contrast between the two couples is stark. Amelia is terrified for George. Rebecca is thrilled by the excitement and the social climbing opportunities. George, rapidly tiring of Amelia’s clingy devotion, becomes infatuated with Rebecca’s sparkle. He gambles heavily and flirts openly with Rebecca at the balls.

At the Duchess of Richmond's ball, on the eve of the Battle of Waterloo, George humiliates Amelia by ignoring her. He spends the evening with Rebecca.

    "George, suffering from the fumes of wine, and the excitement of the play... was entirely under the spell of the little sorceress."

George presses a note into Rebecca's hand asking her to run away with him. Rebecca accepts the note but makes no promise. The drums beat, signaling the advance of the French. The officers must leave immediately.

George, sobered by the imminent battle, returns to his room. He looks at the sleeping Amelia and feels a pang of remorse. He leaves the note from Rebecca in a bouquet, unseen by Amelia, and departs.

The Battle of Waterloo takes place. The narrative focuses on the civilians left in Brussels. Jos Sedley, terrified by rumors of a French victory, attempts to flee. He shaves off his mustache to disguise himself. Rebecca, calm and pragmatic, exploits the panic. She sells her horses to Jos for an exorbitant sum, securing a small fortune in cash.

The battle concludes.

    "No more firing was heard at Brussels—the pursuit rolled miles away. Darkness came down on the field and city: and Amelia was praying for George, who was lying on his face, dead, with a bullet through his heart."

Post-Waterloo:

Amelia returns to England, a widow, and gives birth to a son, Georgy. She lives in poverty with her parents. She devotes her entire existence to the memory of George, whom she idolizes as a saint, and to her son. Dobbin, who supports the Sedleys financially without their knowledge (posing as legal complications regarding the bankruptcy to transfer funds), remains devoted to Amelia but is treated with indifference.

Rebecca and Rawdon spend time in Paris, where Rebecca becomes a sensation in society. They eventually return to London. They have a son, little Rawdon, whom Rebecca neglects entirely.

    "She had not the slightest wish to see him... 'He is a little kitchen-maid's child,' she said."

Rawdon, conversely, develops a deep affection for the boy.

The narrative details "How to Live on Nothing a Year." Rebecca and Rawdon live in a fine house in Mayfair, run up massive debts, and pay no one. Rebecca manages this through charm, manipulation, and the patronage of powerful men.

The most significant patron is the Marquis of Steyne, a wealthy, cynical, and powerful aristocrat. Steyne provides Rebecca with money, jewels, and access to the highest levels of society. Rawdon is largely excluded from these interactions, believing Steyne is merely a benevolent, if high-ranking, friend.

Rebecca reaches the pinnacle of her social climb. She is presented at Court to the King. She participates in a game of charades at a grand party at Gaunt House (Steyne’s residence), playing the roles of Clytemnestra and a Philomela figure, acting out murder and betrayal to applause.

    "The applause was universal... Lord Steyne was in ecstasies... 'She is a little devil,' he said."

The crash comes suddenly. Rawdon is arrested for a debt of roughly 150 pounds on his way home from a party. He is taken to a spunging-house (a prelude to debtor's prison). He writes to Rebecca, begging her to come and bail him out.

Rebecca writes back, claiming she is sick and has no money, but promises to try to raise it the next day. Rawdon, suspicious, sends a message to his brother’s wife, Lady Jane, who comes immediately and pays his debt.

Rawdon returns to his home in Curzon Street unexpectedly. He finds Rebecca alone with Lord Steyne. Rebecca is dressed in jewels she claimed she did not have. Champagne and supper are laid out.

    "Rawdon opened the door and went in... Steyne rose up, grinding his teeth, pale, and with fury in his looks... Becky admired him, in her heart, even at that moment."

Rebecca claims she is innocent. Steyne, believing Rebecca organized the trap to extort him, explodes in rage.

    "'You innocent! Why, every trinket you have on your body is paid for by me... You lie as naturally as you breathe.'"

Rawdon seizes Steyne. He tears the diamond ornament from Rebecca’s breast and flings it at the Lord, cutting his forehead.

    "He struck the peer twice over the face with his open hand, and flung him bleeding to the ground."

Rawdon demands satisfaction (a duel) and throws Rebecca's keys at her, demanding she open her desk. He finds the bank-notes Rebecca had concealed from him—money she could have used to bail him out. He leaves her.

Rebecca is ruined. Rawdon takes a post as Governor of Coventry Island (arranged by Steyne to get him out of the country) and leaves his son in the care of Lady Jane. Rebecca disappears from London society.

Meanwhile, the narrative shifts back to Amelia. Her father, John Sedley, dies. Her mother dies. She lives in genteel poverty. Old Osborne, desperate for an heir, offers to take Georgy and raise him as a gentleman, provided Amelia gives him up. Initially, she refuses. However, seeing that she cannot provide for him and that he is suffering from their poverty, she makes the "great sacrifice."

    "She gave up the child to his grandfather... She saw the boy's box carried away... and she went into her room and knelt down and prayed."

Georgy becomes a spoiled miniature of his father in the Osborne household. Dobbin returns from India, where he has been for years. He visits Amelia. He finds her faded but still worshipping the memory of George. Dobbin attempts to reconcile the factions. He brings Georgy back to see his mother.

Old Osborne eventually dies, leaving a will that acknowledges Georgy as the heir and restores a portion of the fortune to the Sedley family (thanks to Dobbin's intervention). Amelia is now wealthy. She, Georgy, Jos (who has returned), and Dobbin travel to the continent.
C. Finale (Conclusion)

The group travels to the Grand Duchy of Pumpernickel (Weimar). Here, they encounter a woman living in a boarding house, calling herself Madame de Raudon. It is Rebecca. She has spent years wandering Europe, gambling, drinking, and living in disreputable circumstances, rejected by respectable society.

Jos discovers her. Rebecca instantly adopts the role of the persecuted victim. She tells Jos a fabricated story of how her son was torn from her and how she has been wronged by the world. Jos is once again ensnared. He brings her to see Amelia.

Amelia, still naive and kind-hearted, welcomes Rebecca. Dobbin, however, knows Rebecca's history and character. He vehemently opposes her re-entry into their lives.

    "I will not have it," Dobbin said... "She is a woman who has no heart and no conscience... I will not have her in the house."

Amelia accuses Dobbin of cruelty. She invokes the memory of her "saintly" husband, George, claiming that George would have supported her charity. This breaks Dobbin. After nearly twenty years of unrequited devotion, he finally confronts Amelia with the truth of her own selfishness.

    "You have used me, and you have bullied me... You are not worthy of the love which I have devoted to you... I bid you goodbye."

Dobbin leaves Pumpernickel. Amelia is shocked by his departure but remains stubborn. Rebecca, now installed in the household, realizes that Dobbin’s departure endangers her own security; she needs the stability of the group to secure Jos. She sees that Amelia is pining for Dobbin but refuses to admit it.

In a rare moment of what appears to be either pity or pragmatic calculation, Rebecca decides to shatter Amelia’s illusion about George Osborne. Amelia speaks of George’s purity.

    "You are a fool," Rebecca said... "You think he loved you... He was tired of you... He was ready to run away with me."

Amelia denies it. Rebecca produces the physical evidence: the note George slipped to her at the ball before Waterloo, asking her to flee with him.

    "Becky... opened the desk... and held out a slip of paper... 'Look at that,' she said."

Amelia reads the note. The idol is smashed. "There was nothing to forbid her now to love William Dobbin."

Amelia writes to Dobbin. He returns. They are reconciled.

    "The vessel is in port. He has got the prize he has been trying for all his life."

Dobbin and Amelia marry. However, the narrative suggests a muted happiness. Dobbin realizes the prize was perhaps not worth the lifetime of waiting.

    "Grow green again, tender little parasite, round the rugged old oak to which you cling."

Final Outcomes:

Joseph Sedley remains under Rebecca’s influence. He lives in terror of her. He takes out a large life insurance policy. He dies at Aix-la-Chapelle in suspicious circumstances soon after.

    "The money was left between his mother and sister... but the insurance money... was left to—Madame de Rawdon."

Rebecca settles down. She becomes a "virtuous" woman, engaging in charitable works and writing hymns. She lives in Bath and Cheltenham.

    "She busies herself in works of piety. She goes to church, and never without a footman... Her name is in all the Charity Lists."

The narrator concludes the performance by putting away the puppets.

    "Ah! Vanitas Vanitatum! Which of us is happy in this world? Which of us has his desire? or, having it, is satisfied? Come, children, let us shut up the box and the puppets, for our play is played out."
