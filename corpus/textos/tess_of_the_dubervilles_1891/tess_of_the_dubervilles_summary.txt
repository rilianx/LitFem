2. Comprehensive Plot Summary (Written Order Only)
A. Introduction
The narrative commences on a road in the Vale of Blackmoor, late May. John Durbeyfield, a haggler, is walking home when he is accosted by Parson Tringham. The Parson addresses him as "Sir John" and reveals that Durbeyfield is the lineal representative of the ancient and knightly family of the d'Urbervilles. Tringham explains the lineage:

"Your ancestor was one of the twelve knights who assisted the Lord of Estremavilla in Normandy in his conquest of Glamorganshire. Branches of your family held manors over all this part of England... You are extinct—as a county family."

Durbeyfield, emboldened by this genealogical revelation, sends for a horse and carriage to transport him the rest of the way to Marlott.

In Marlott, the village women participate in the May-Day dance (Club-walking). Tess Durbeyfield, John’s daughter, is present, dressed in white. Three brothers, the Clares, observe the dance while passing through on a walking tour. The youngest, Angel Clare, joins the dance but chooses a partner other than Tess. As he leaves to rejoin his brothers, he looks back and notices Tess, realizing he overlooked her.

John Durbeyfield arrives home drunk. The family learns of the d'Urberville connection. Joan Durbeyfield, Tess's mother, reveals her plan to send Tess to Trantridge to claim kin with a wealthy Mrs. d'Urberville, hoping for financial assistance or a marriage match.

The following morning, the family's horse, Prince, is required to transport beehives to market because John is incapacitated by drink. Tess and her younger brother Abraham undertake the journey at 1:00 AM. Tess falls asleep while holding the reins. The mail-cart speeds towards them; the shaft enters Prince’s chest, killing the horse instantly. Tess attempts to stop the bleeding with her hand but fails.

"The pointed shaft of the cart had entered the breast of the unhappy Prince like a sword, and from the wound his life’s blood was spouting in a stream, and falling with a hiss into the road."

Tess regards herself as a murderess. The loss of the horse cripples the family’s livelihood. To make restitution, Tess agrees to her mother's plan and travels to The Slopes, the d'Urberville estate near Trantridge. She does not know that the family there—the Stokes—adopted the d'Urberville name to hide their industrial origins.

Tess meets Alec d'Urberville, a young man who is smoking in the garden. He is struck by her appearance. He guides her through the grounds and offers her strawberries. When she declines to take them from his hand, he insists on feeding her physically.

"He stood up and held it by the stem to her mouth. 'No—no!' she said quickly, putting her fingers between his hand and her lips. 'I would rather take it in my own hand.' 'Nonsense!' he insisted; and in a slight distress she parted her lips and took it in."

Alec arranges for Tess to manage the poultry farm at The Slopes. She returns to Marlott to prepare. Her mother dresses her in her best clothes, and she departs for Trantridge, unaware of Alec's intentions or her parents’ specific matrimonial hopes.

B. The Body (Rising Action)
The Residence at Trantridge Tess begins work at the poultry house. Alec d'Urberville repeatedly intrudes upon her work, teaching her to whistle to the bullfinches. On a Saturday night in September, Tess walks to the market town of Chaseborough with other Trantridge workers. She waits for her friends to walk home, but the group becomes intoxicated. On the return journey, a confrontation erupts between Tess and a woman named Car Darch (the "Queen of Spades") regarding treacle impacting a dress. The workers turn hostile toward Tess.

Alec d'Urberville rides up and rescues Tess from the mob, lifting her onto his horse. He rides away from the group, ostensibly to take her home, but diverts into The Chase, an ancient woodland. The fog thickens. Alec claims he is lost and dismounts to find the path, leaving Tess on a bed of dead leaves. He gives her his coat. Exhausted by the night's events and the ride, Tess falls asleep.

Alec returns to find her sleeping. The narrative records the darkness and silence of the environment before stating the event:

"Darkness and silence ruled everywhere around. Above them rose the primeval yews and oaks of The Chase, in which were poised gentle roosting birds in their last nap... But, might some say, where was Tess’s guardian angel? where was the providence of her simple faith?"

The Return to Marlott Some weeks later, Tess leaves Trantridge. Alec drives her part of the way, acknowledging that he has "confused" her. Tess rejects his offers of further maintenance. She returns to her parents' house. The villagers notice her changed condition. She gives birth to a boy. She works in the fields while the baby is kept indoors.

The infant becomes ill. Tess, fearing for the child's soul, decides to perform a baptism herself when her father refuses to allow the parson into the house. She wakes her younger siblings to act as the congregation. She pours water over the child and names him.

"She did not look like Sissy to them now, but as a being large, towering, and awful—a divine personage with whom they had nothing in common. She pronounced it: 'SORROW, I baptize thee in the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Ghost.'"

The child dies shortly after. The parson refuses a Christian burial, so Tess buries Sorrow in a corner of the churchyard reserved for drunkards and suicides, placing a homemade cross and a jar of flowers on the grave.

Talbothays Dairy Tess remains at home for some time, then seeks employment at a dairy farm far to the south, in the Valley of the Great Dairies, where she is unknown. She arrives at Talbothays Dairy in May. The environment is lush and fertile. She begins work as a milkmaid for Dairyman Crick.

She discovers that Angel Clare is learning farming at the dairy. He is the son of a vicar but has rejected the church due to dogmatic differences. They do not initially speak often, but proximity in the milking shed and the shared atmosphere of the season draw them together.

Tess hears Angel playing his harp in the garden at dusk. She listens from the uncultivated edge of the garden, moving through weeds and dampness to remain hidden.

"The floating pollen seemed to be his notes made visible, and the dampness of the garden the weeping of the garden's sensibility. Though near nightfall, the rank-smelling weed-flowers glowed as if they would not close for intentness, and the waves of colour mixed with the waves of sound."

Angel discovers her. They begin to converse regularly. They wake early for the morning milking, often being the only two people awake in the "spectral, half-compounded, aqueous light." The relationship deepens during the summer. Angel idealizes Tess as a fresh, virginal daughter of nature, calling her "Artemis" and "Demeter." Tess tries to steer him toward the other dairymaids (Marian, Izz, Retty) due to her past, but Angel is fixated on her.

The Courtship and Confession Attempts Angel travels to his family home at Emminster. He discusses his marriage plans with his parents, who want him to marry a pure, Christian woman named Mercy Chant. Angel argues for Tess, a "daughter of the soil." He returns to Talbothays and proposes to Tess.

Tess refuses multiple times, citing her unworthiness but refusing to explain why. She eventually accepts, unable to resist her affection for him.

"She had consented. She might as well have agreed at first. The 'appetite for joy' which pervades all creation... had at last mastered Tess."

As the wedding date approaches, Tess attempts to tell Angel the truth about her past. She writes a four-page letter detailing her history with Alec and slips it under his door. On the morning of the wedding, she discovers the letter slipped under the carpet, unopened. Angel has not read it. She attempts to tell him verbally, but he dismisses her concerns, telling her there will be time for confession after the marriage.

The Wedding and Revelation They marry. During the ceremony, Tess claims to see the d'Urberville coach (an ill omen). They travel to Wellbridge Flour-Mills for their honeymoon, staying in an old d'Urberville mansion.

That evening, Angel confesses that he had a brief dissipating affair with a stranger in London before he came to the dairy. He asks for her forgiveness. Tess, seeing this as an opportunity, forgives him instantly and then offers her own confession, believing the situations are equal. She recounts the events at Trantridge.

Angel’s demeanor changes instantly. The fire in the grate goes out. He refuses to accept the parallel between their situations.

"You were one person; now you are another. My God—how can forgiveness meet such a grotesque—prestidigitation as that!"

Tess accepts his judgment without argument. She offers to drown herself or let him divorce her. Angel says divorce is impossible due to the law. They sleep separately.

Sleepwalking, Angel enters Tess's room, picks her up, and carries her across a narrow footbridge over the river Froom. He lays her in an empty stone coffin at the ruined abbey, murmuring "Dead! dead! dead!" Tess does not wake him, fearing he might fall if startled. He carries her back to the house. The next morning, he treats the night's events as if they did not happen.

The Separation Angel decides to leave England for Brazil to farm. He gives Tess some money and instructs her not to join him until he sends for her. He drops her off near her village. He visits his parents, then departs.

Tess gives her money to her parents to repair their roof and travels to seek work. She ends up at Flintcomb-Ash, a starve-acre farm on high, harsh chalkland. The work is brutal. She hacks swedes in the winter rain and snow. She is joined by Marian and Izz Huett, former dairymaids from Talbothays, who have turned to drink and depression since Angel’s marriage.

The Return of Alec Tess travels to Emminster to visit Angel’s parents but loses her courage when she overhears Angel’s brothers discussing Angel's "ill-considered marriage." On her return walk, she encounters a preaching barn. The preacher is Alec d'Urberville, who has been converted to Christianity by Angel’s father.

Alec sees Tess and stops preaching. He pursues her, claiming her presence weakens his new faith. He asks her to swear on a cross that she will not tempt him; she swears. He later learns the cross marks the site of a malefactor's execution.

Alec abandons his preaching. He begins stalking Tess at Flintcomb-Ash. He proposes that she live with him, mocking her marriage to an absent husband.

The Threshing The threshing machine arrives at Flintcomb-Ash. Tess is assigned the grueling task of feeding the machine on the platform. The work is relentless.

"The long chimney running up beside an ash-tree... explained that here was the engine which was to act as the primum mobile of this little world... The threshing-machine which, whilst it was going, kept up a despotic demand upon the endurance of their muscles and nerves."

Alec appears during the work and offers to take her and her family away from the toil. He strikes her with a glove; she strikes him with a leather glove. He leaves but continues to pressure her.

Tess writes a desperate letter to Angel in Brazil: "Come to me—come to me, and save me from what threatens me!"

The Eviction John Durbeyfield dies. The family loses their life-hold on the cottage. They are evicted and load their possessions onto a wagon, traveling to Kingsbere, the site of the d'Urberville tombs. They find they have no lodgings. They set up a temporary shelter under the church wall, utilizing the d'Urberville window for a tent.

Alec d'Urberville tracks them down. He tells Tess that Angel will never return and offers housing for her mother and siblings if she returns to him. Tess looks at the entrance to the d'Urberville vault and wishes she were inside. She forces her sister Liza-Lu to write to Angel, as she has ceased hoping.

C. Finale (Conclusion)
Angel's Return Angel Clare returns from Brazil, emaciated and ill. He has read Tess's letter and regretted his harshness. He searches for her at the Durbeyfield home but finds strangers. He tracks Joan Durbeyfield, who is living comfortably. She is evasive about Tess's location but eventually admits she is in Sandbourne.

Angel travels to Sandbourne, a fashionable seaside resort. He finds the address, a lodging house called The Herons. Tess appears in the drawing room, dressed in expensive cashmere and silk. She stands before him.

"He had never seen her as she was now, smiling—with a smile which sat ill on her... 'It is too late,' she said, her voice sounding hard through the room. 'I waited and waited for you. But you did not come! And I wrote to you, and you did not come! He kept on saying you would never come any more, and that I was a foolish woman. He was very kind to me, and to mother, and to all of us after father's death.'"

She tells Angel to go away, stating that Alec d'Urberville has won her back. Angel leaves, dazed.

The Murder Mrs. Brooks, the landlady, listens at the keyhole. She hears Tess arguing with Alec.

"And then my husband came home, which I never expected him to do... and you taunted me, and said what was not true!"

Silence follows. Tess leaves the house. Mrs. Brooks enters the room and sees a spot of red on the ceiling. She finds Alec d'Urberville stabbed to death in the bed.

"The dead silence within was broken only by a regular beat. Drip, drip, drip."

The Flight Angel is walking out of Sandbourne when Tess runs after him. She confesses she has killed Alec.

"I have killed him... I killed him just now in his room... I feared long ago, when I struck him on the mouth with my glove, that I might do it... He has come between us and ruined us, and now he can never do it any more."

Angel, realizing she is in a state of delirium but speaking the truth, accepts her. He decides to protect her. They walk inland, avoiding main roads. They break into an empty mansion, Bramshurst Court, and stay there for five days in "absolute seclusion." They finally leave when the caretaker arrives.

Stonehenge They walk north in the darkness, reaching Salisbury Plain. They stumble upon Stonehenge. Tess, exhausted, lies down on one of the stones (the Altar Stone). She feels at home among the "heathen" ruins.

"Prophet and priest wind-whistle shorn. The exact response to the surroundings." (Note: This is an atmospheric description, not dialogue).

She asks Angel if he believes they will meet again after death. He does not answer. She sleeps. Dawn breaks. Angel sees figures approaching from all sides—the police. He realizes they are surrounded. He asks the officers to let her finish her sleep.

Tess wakes and sees the men. She stands up.

"'It is as it should be,' she murmured. 'Angel, I am almost glad—yes, glad! This happiness could not have lasted. It was too much. I have had enough; and now I shall not live for you to despise me!' She stood up, shook herself, and went forward, neither of the men having moved. 'I am ready,' she said quietly."

Wintoncester The narrative shifts to Wintoncester (Winchester). Angel Clare and Liza-Lu (Tess's younger sister) are walking up a hill outside the city. They look down at the prison. A black flag is raised on the tower, signaling that the execution has been carried out.

The narrator concludes the record with the following statement:

"The two speechless gazers bent themselves down to the earth, as if in prayer, and remained thus a long time, absolutely motionless: the flag continued to wave silently. As soon as they had strength they arose, joined hands again, and went on."

The text ends with a final comment on the nature of the tragedy:

"'Justice' was done, and the President of the Immortals, in Aeschylean phrase, had ended his sport with Tess."
