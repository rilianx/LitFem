1. Narrator & Characters

Narrator Metadata

Narrator Voice: Third-person omniscient (with occasional first-person intrusion as the Surveyor of the Custom-House).
Narrator Perspective: Retrospective and historical; claims to be editing a manuscript found in the Salem Custom House.
Narrator Type: Editor-Narrator; mediates the story through the persona of a discoverer of the "scarlet letter" artifact.
Knowledge Scope: Omniscient regarding characters' thoughts, feelings, and private actions; limited by the claimed historical record and occasional admitted ambiguity regarding supernatural events.
Narrative Mediation: The narrative is presented as a reconstruction of events that occurred approximately two hundred years prior to the narrator's time, filtered through the "surveyor's" perspective.

Main Characters

["Hester Prynne (f)", "Arthur Dimmesdale (m)", "Roger Chillingworth (m)", "Pearl (f)"]
2. Comprehensive Plot Summary
A. Introduction

The narrative commences with a description of a crowd assembled in front of a wooden edifice in Boston, during the mid-seventeenth century. The text identifies the building as the prison. Beside the prison door stands a wild rose-bush. The narrator notes that this rose-bush has reportedly "sprung up under the footsteps of the sainted Ann Hutchinson," offering fragrance to the prisoner.

The crowd waits. A group of women discuss the impending punishment. One woman, described as "hard-featured," advocates for execution, stating, "At the very least, they should have put the brand of a hot iron on Hester Prynne’s forehead." Another woman suggests the current punishment is sufficient because "the pang of it will be always in her heart."

The town beadle emerges, leading Hester Prynne. She carries a three-month-old infant in her arms. On the breast of her gown, in fine red cloth surrounded with elaborate embroidery and fantastic flourishes of gold thread, appears the letter "A." The narrator records that Hester repels the beadle’s hand and steps into the open air with "natural dignity and force of character."

She is led to the market-place and ascends a scaffold. She is sentenced to stand there for three hours. The crowd stares. The narrator details Hester's recollection of her past: a "decayed house of gray stone" in England, a "paternal home," and the face of a man "well stricken in years, a pale, thin, scholar-like visage" with eyes dim and bleared by lamplight. She also recalls his physical deformity, having a "left shoulder a trifle higher than the right."

While standing on the scaffold, Hester observes a man on the outskirts of the crowd. He is dressed in a strange mixture of civilized and savage costume. He is small in stature and possesses the uneven shoulders Hester just recalled. This man signals Hester to remain silent by "laying his finger on his lips."

The man asks a townsman who the woman is. The townsman explains she is Hester Prynne, the wife of a learned man who sent her ahead to Massachusetts while he remained in Amsterdam. She has been in the colony for two years without news of him. The townsman reveals: "She hath raised a great scandal... in our godly New England." The stranger inquires about the father of the child. The townsman replies that the "guilty partner" remains unknown. The stranger responds:

    "He will be known! He will be known! He will be known!"

The Governor, Bellingham, and the Reverend John Wilson, along with the Reverend Arthur Dimmesdale, occupy a balcony overlooking the scaffold. Wilson calls upon Dimmesdale to exhort Hester to confess. Dimmesdale, described as young and possessing a "striking aspect" of apprehension, leans over the balcony. He addresses Hester:

    "I charge thee to speak out the name of thy fellow-sinner and fellow-sufferer! Be not silent from any mistaken pity and tenderness for him... Take heed how thou deniest to him—who, perchance, hath not the courage to grasp it for himself—the bitter, but wholesome, cup that is now presented to thy lips!"

Hester shakes her head. She replies, "Never! ... And would that I might endure his agony, as well as mine!" The Reverend Wilson attempts to persuade her, but she refuses to speak. The child cries. After the sermon, Hester is returned to the prison.

Inside the prison, the jailer summons a physician for the agitated infant and mother. The stranger from the crowd enters, identified as Roger Chillingworth. He administers a draught to the child, then prepares one for Hester. She fears poison. He assures her:

    "Even if I imagine a scheme of vengeance, what could I do better for my object than to let thee live... so that this burning shame may still blaze upon thy bosom?"

Hester drinks. Chillingworth reveals he is her husband. He admits his folly in marrying a young woman when he was old and bookish. He asks for the name of her lover. Hester refuses. Chillingworth exacts a promise from her: she must not reveal his identity as her husband to the town. He warns:

    "Recognize me not, by word, by sign, by look! Breathe not the secret, above all, to the man thou wottest of. Shouldst thou fail me in this, beware! ... Thy fame, thy position, thy life, will not be shrunk from me."

Hester asks if he is the "Black Man" (the devil). Chillingworth replies, "Not thy soul... No, not thine!"
B. The Body (Rising Action)

Hester is released from prison. She does not leave the settlement, though the narrator notes she is free to return to Europe. She settles in a small, abandoned cottage on the outskirts of town, looking across the basin of the sea.

She supports herself and the child through needlework. Her work becomes fashionable; she creates ruffs, bands, and funeral robes for the magistrates and community, though never bridal veils. The text states:

    "Her needle-work was seen on the ruff of the Governor; military men wore it on their scarfs, and the minister on his band; it decked the baby’s little cap; it was shut up, to be mildewed and moulder away, in the coffins of the dead."

Hester remains isolated. The community insults her; clergy preach sermons about her in the street. She suspects the letter gives her a "sympathetic knowledge" of hidden sin in others’ hearts.

The child is named Pearl. The narrator describes Pearl as having a "native grace" and "faultless beauty" but also a chaotic, ungovernable spirit. Hester dresses Pearl in crimson velvet tunics that resemble the scarlet letter. Pearl displays a fascination with the letter on her mother's chest, sometimes throwing wildflowers at it. When Hester tries to teach Pearl catechism, the child asserts she "had not been made at all, but had been plucked by her mother off the bush of wild roses that grew by the prison-door."

Hester hears rumors that the magistrates plan to take Pearl away, arguing that if the child is a demon, she endangers Hester’s soul, and if she is human, Hester is unfit to raise her. Hester goes to Governor Bellingham’s mansion to plead her case.

At the mansion, Hester and Pearl encounter the Governor, Reverend Wilson, Roger Chillingworth, and Arthur Dimmesdale. Chillingworth has changed; his features have become uglier and his complexion darker. The Governor asks Pearl, "Who made thee?" Pearl refuses to answer initially, then claims she was plucked from the rose-bush.

The Governor decides to remove the child. Hester violently protests, declaring:

    "God gave me the child! He gave her in requital of all things else, which ye had taken from me... Ye shall not take her! I will die first!"

Hester appeals to Dimmesdale for help. "Speak for me!" she cries. "Thou wast my pastor, and hadst charge of my soul, and knowest me better than these men can."

Dimmesdale steps forward. He argues that God gave the child to Hester to keep her soul alive through the "torture" of the child’s presence. He states:

    "It was meant for a blessing; for the one blessing of her life! It was meant, doubtless, as the mother herself hath told us, for a retribution too; a torture to be felt at many an unthought-of moment..."

The magistrates accept Dimmesdale's argument. Pearl approaches Dimmesdale, takes his hand, and lays her cheek against it. Dimmesdale kisses her brow. Chillingworth observes this interaction closely, noting, "The little baggage hath witchcraft in her, I profess."

As Hester leaves, Mistress Hibbins (the Governor’s sister, later executed as a witch) invites Hester to a forest meeting with the "Black Man." Hester declines, saying she must take care of Pearl, but adds that if they had taken the child, she would have gone.

The narrative shifts to the relationship between Dimmesdale and Chillingworth. Dimmesdale’s health is failing; he is pale and emaciated, often placing his hand over his heart. The townspeople believe his decline is due to his extreme holiness and labor. Chillingworth, presenting himself as a physician, attaches himself to the minister. The town arranges for them to lodge in the same house so Chillingworth can monitor Dimmesdale’s health.

Chillingworth spends hours probing Dimmesdale’s mind, seeking the root of his physical malady. The narrator describes Chillingworth as a miner searching for gold in the minister's soul.

One day, Chillingworth brings in dark, flabby weeds. He tells Dimmesdale he found them growing on a grave with no tombstone, suggesting they grew out of the dead man’s heart as a sign of unconfessed secret sins. Dimmesdale argues that some men must keep their sins secret to continue their service to God. Chillingworth counters that a "pure hand" is needed for worship.

Dimmesdale becomes agitated and rushes out. Later, Dimmesdale falls into a deep sleep at noon. Chillingworth enters the room, approaches the sleeping minister, and pulls aside the vestment on Dimmesdale's chest. The narrator does not describe what Chillingworth sees, but records his reaction:

    "But what distinguished the physician’s ecstasy from Satan’s was the trait of wonder in it!"

After this, Chillingworth’s scrutiny becomes more malevolent. He plays on Dimmesdale’s conscience. Dimmesdale’s popularity as a preacher grows as his suffering increases; his "Tongue of Flame" allows him to speak to the sinners in his congregation with empathy. He attempts to confess in the pulpit, calling himself "the vilest of sinners," but the congregation only reveres him more.

Privately, Dimmesdale practices self-flagellation with a scourge. He fasts until his knees tremble. He keeps nightly vigils.

One night in early May, Dimmesdale walks to the scaffold where Hester stood seven years prior. He ascends the platform in the darkness. Overcome by a "great horror of mind," he screams aloud. No one comes, though Governor Bellingham and Mistress Hibbins look out their windows.

Reverend Wilson passes by with a lantern but does not see Dimmesdale. Then, Hester and Pearl approach. They are returning from the deathbed of Governor Winthrop. Dimmesdale invites them to ascend the scaffold:

    "Come up hither, Hester, thou and little Pearl... Ye have both been here before, but I was not with you. Come up hither once again, and we will stand all three together!"

They join him. Pearl asks, "Minister! ... Wilt thou stand here with mother and me, to-morrow noontide?" Dimmesdale answers, "Not so, my child... but another time." He says they will stand together "At the great judgment day."

A meteor flashes across the sky, illuminating the street. The narrator describes the light as revealing the cloud formations, which to Dimmesdale’s eyes appear to form a gigantic letter "A" in red light. Pearl points across the street. Roger Chillingworth stands there, watching them.

Dimmesdale is terrified. "Who is that man, Hester?" he gasps. Hester, bound by her oath, remains silent. Chillingworth approaches, claiming he found the minister sleepwalking, and leads him home.

The next day, the sexton hands Dimmesdale a black glove found on the scaffold, saying "Satan dropped it there." The sexton also mentions the meteor, saying the town interpreted the letter "A" to stand for "Angel," marking Governor Winthrop’s ascent to heaven.

The narrative focuses on Hester. Seven years have passed. Her reputation has evolved. Her active benevolence and aid to the sick have changed the meaning of the letter in the eyes of many. The narrator states:

    "they said that it meant Able; so strong was Hester Prynne, with a woman’s strength."

However, Hester herself has become austere. Her hair is hidden under a cap; her beauty has seemingly vanished behind the "marble coldness" of her life. She resolves to help Dimmesdale, perceiving that he is on the verge of lunacy due to Chillingworth’s influence.

Hester confronts Chillingworth near the shore where he is gathering herbs. She observes that he has transformed into a wretched, misshapen figure with red light in his eyes. She tells him she plans to break her promise and reveal his identity to Dimmesdale.

Chillingworth admits he has become a fiend. He recalls his old self as a scholar. He claims the events are a "dark necessity." He tells Hester:

    "Ye that have wronged me are not sinful, save in a kind of typical illusion; neither am I fiend-like, who have snatched a fiend’s office from his hands. It is our fate. Let the black flower blossom as it may!"

Hester walks in the forest with Pearl, waiting for Dimmesdale to return from a visit to a persistent apostle to the Indians. Pearl asks about the "Black Man" and why the minister keeps his hand over his heart. Hester tells Pearl to go play.

Dimmesdale approaches, walking with a listless gait. Hester calls to him. They sit on a mossy bank. They touch hands, described as "chill as death." They verify each other’s corporal existence.

Dimmesdale asks, "Hester, hast thou found peace?" She looks at the scarlet letter and answers, "Hast thou?" He replies, "None!—nothing but despair!" He confesses his misery and the hypocrisy of his position.

Hester reveals the truth:

    "O Arthur... forgive me! In all things else, I have striven to be true! ... That old man!—the physician!—he whom they call Roger Chillingworth!—he was my husband!"

Dimmesdale sinks to the ground, claiming he cannot forgive her. Hester embraces him, pressing his face against the scarlet letter. Eventually, he says, "I do forgive thee, Hester."

They discuss the danger of Chillingworth. Hester urges Dimmesdale to leave the settlement. "Go!" she says. "Wander! ... Preach! ... The world is all before thee!" Dimmesdale claims he lacks the strength to go alone.

Hester whispers, "Thou shalt not go alone!"

Dimmesdale resolves to leave. In the excitement of this decision, Hester removes the scarlet letter from her breast and throws it among the withered leaves. She takes off her cap, letting her dark, rich hair fall down. The narrator describes a "flood of sunshine" filling the forest.

Hester calls Pearl to join them. Pearl refuses to cross the brook. She points to Hester’s chest. Hester realizes she must replace the letter to pacify the child. She retrieves the letter, pins it on, and tucks her hair back under her cap. The sunshine fades. Pearl crosses the brook and kisses Hester, and also kisses the scarlet letter.

They plan to take a ship to Bristol in four days. Dimmesdale returns to town. As he walks, he experiences strange impulses: he wants to whisper blasphemies to a deacon, argue against the immortality of the soul to a widow, and teach wicked words to Puritan children. He encounters Mistress Hibbins, who suggests she knows he has been to the forest.

Dimmesdale reaches his home. Chillingworth offers him medicine. Dimmesdale refuses, saying he no longer needs it. He throws his old Election Sermon into the fire and writes a new one, working through the night with "impulsive flow of thought and emotion."
C. Finale (Conclusion)

The day of the New England Holiday (Election Day) arrives. Hester and Pearl enter the market-place. Hester wears coarse gray cloth; the letter burns on her bosom. Pearl is dressed in bright attire.

The commander of the Spanish vessel that is to take them to Europe speaks to Hester. He informs her that another passenger has joined the party.

    "Chillingworth... he tells me he is of your party, and a close friend to the gentleman you spoke of—he that is in peril."

Hester looks up and sees Chillingworth smiling at her from across the market-place.

The procession begins. The magistrates and soldiers march, followed by the Reverend Dimmesdale. The narrator notes that Dimmesdale exhibits a tremendous energy and spiritual abstraction. He does not look at Hester. Pearl asks, "Is not this the same minister that kissed me by the brook?" Hester scolds her to be quiet.

Mistress Hibbins approaches Hester and speaks of the minister’s mark. "What is it, good Mistress Prynne, which you wear on your bosom?—and what is that which the minister seeks to hide, with his hand always over his heart?"

Dimmesdale enters the meeting-house to deliver the Election Sermon. Hester stands near the scaffold. The crowd is dense. The sermon is described as having a deep, mournful undertone of human agony.

After the sermon, the procession exits the church. The crowd shouts in applause for the minister. Dimmesdale appears, pale and tottering. He reaches the scaffold where Hester stands. He pauses. The Governor attempts to assist him, but Dimmesdale repels him.

He turns to Hester and Pearl. "Come, Hester, come, support me up yonder scaffold!"

Chillingworth catches Dimmesdale’s arm. He whispers:

    "Madman, hold! What is your purpose? ... All shall be well! Do not blacken your fame, and perish in dishonour! I can yet save you!"

Dimmesdale replies, "Ha, tempter! Methinks thou art too late! ... Thy power is not what it was! With God’s help, I shall escape thee now!"

Dimmesdale ascends the scaffold, leaning on Hester. He stands where he stood seven years ago. He addresses the multitude:

    "People of New England! ... ye, that have loved me!—ye, that have deemed me holy!—behold me here, the one sinner of the world! At last!—at last!—I stand upon the spot where, seven years since, I should have stood!"

He speaks of the scarlet letter Hester wears. He claims he carries a similar mark.

    "It was on him! God’s eye beheld it! The angels were for ever pointing at it! The Devil knew it well, and fretted it with the touch of his burning finger!"

Dimmesdale tears away the ministerial band from before his breast. The narrator records: "It was revealed!" The text does not explicitly describe what is revealed, but notes the crowd's horror.

Dimmesdale sinks to his knees. Chillingworth kneels beside him, his face described as blank and dull. Chillingworth repeats, "Thou hast escaped me! Thou hast escaped me!" Dimmesdale replies, "May God forgive thee! Thou, too, hast deeply sinned!"

Dimmesdale asks Pearl for a kiss. Pearl kisses his lips. The narrator states: "A spell was broken... her tears fell upon her father’s cheek."

Hester asks, "Shall we not meet again? ... Shall we not spend our immortal life together?"

Dimmesdale replies:

    "Hush, Hester, hush! ... The law we broke!—the sin here so awfully revealed! ... It may be, that, when we forgot our God—when we violated our reverence each for the other’s soul—it was thenceforth vain to hope that we could meet hereafter... Praised be his name! His will be done! Farewell!"

Dimmesdale dies.

The narrator discusses the various accounts of what was seen on Dimmesdale’s chest. Most witnesses testify to seeing a Scarlet Letter "the very semblance of that worn by Hester Prynne," imprinted in the flesh. Some claim he inflicted it himself; others claim Chillingworth produced it with drugs; others say it was the effect of remorse "gnawing from the inmost heart outwardly." A minority of spectators deny seeing anything at all on his bosom and deny that he confessed to any specific sin.

Roger Chillingworth "withered up, shrivelled away" like an uprooted weed. He dies within a year, bequeathing a large fortune to Pearl. Hester and Pearl disappear. The story of the scarlet letter becomes a legend.

Many years later, Hester returns alone to the cottage. She resumes wearing the scarlet letter, though not compelled by any magistrate. The narrator notes that the letter ceased to be a stigma and became something to be looked upon with awe and reverence. People, especially women, come to her for counsel in their sorrows. Hester assures them that at some brighter period, a "new truth" would be revealed to establish the whole relation between man and woman on a surer ground of mutual happiness.

Hester dies. She is buried in the burial ground near the King’s Chapel. Her grave is near an old and sunken grave (implied to be Dimmesdale's), with a space between them as if "the dust of the two sleepers had no right to mingle."

They share one tombstone. The narrator concludes by describing the device on the slate:

    "On a field, sable, the letter A, gules."
