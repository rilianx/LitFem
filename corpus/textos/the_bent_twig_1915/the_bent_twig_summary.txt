Comprehensive Plot Summary
A. Introduction
The narrative opens with a detailed survey of the Marshall family's residence and their standing in the university town of La Chance. The house is described as distinct from its neighbors, an "unkept" acreage of oak trees and lawn that signals the family's disregard for conventional appearances. The reader encounters the Marshalls through the lens of their difference; they are an academic family living on a meager income, yet they possess a distinct social gravity that confuses the local hierarchy.

Sylvia and Judith Marshall are introduced as children. Sylvia is sensitive, acutely aware of social nuances, and prone to resentment regarding her family's "queerness." Judith is robust, indifferent to opinion, and intellectually precocious. The text establishes the family dynamic: Professor Marshall is a spirited, somewhat chaotic intellectual, while Mrs. Marshall is the serene, grounding force who manages the household with a "noble" economy.

A defining early scene involves the ritual of hair-washing. The narrative records Sylvia’s physical discomfort and her internal monologue regarding the lack of luxury in her life compared to the town’s wealthy elite.

"Sylvia sat on the edge of the bath-tub, her eyes tightly shut, her face screwed into a knot of desperate endurance. She knew that if she opened her eyes the soap would get into them and smart, but it was impossible to keep them shut."

The narrative moves to the "Saturday Night" gatherings at the Marshall home. These events are open to faculty and students alike. The reader observes the mixture of high intellectual discourse and simple refreshments (popcorn and apples), which Sylvia views with a mixture of pride and embarrassment. She observes the guests, noting the way her parents command respect despite their lack of material wealth.

"The air was full of smoke and talk and laughter... Sylvia, looking at them, felt a sudden wave of that inexplicable pride which was the torment of her life. She was proud of her father and mother, and she was ashamed of them."

The "inciting incident" for Sylvia’s character arc—her confrontation with social stratification—occurs during her primary school years. The text documents her interactions with the "aristocracy" of La Chance, specifically the Colonel’s daughters. Sylvia attempts to conform to their standards of dress and behavior but is thwarted by her family’s principles. When Sylvia complains about wearing "sensible" shoes while other girls wear patent leather, Mrs. Marshall responds with a directive that prioritizes function over fashion, a recurring point of contention.

"'My dear child,' said Mrs. Marshall, 'you are not the other girls. You are Sylvia Marshall.'"

The introduction concludes with Sylvia entering her adolescence, carrying a "resentful submission" to her family's eccentricities while harboring a fierce, secret determination to eventually attain the beauty, wealth, and social ease she observes in the "best families" of La Chance.

B. The Body (Rising Action)
The State University Years

Sylvia enters the State University at La Chance. The text documents her immediate rise to prominence due to her developing beauty and natural charisma. Unlike Judith, who focuses entirely on academics, Sylvia navigates the complex social strata of the university. She interacts with the "smart set," a group of wealthy students who dominate the social scene.

The narrative records a specific incident involving Jerry Fiske, a socially awkward student whom Professor Marshall encourages Sylvia to befriend. Sylvia resists, finding him "grimy" and "uninteresting." However, under parental pressure, she invites him to a dance. The event is documented as a social failure for Sylvia, who is ignored by the popular crowd while partnered with Fiske.

"She felt a rage at him, at her father, at herself... She looked at his red, perspiring face and clumsy hands with a hard hostility."

Despite this, Sylvia eventually gains entry into the elite circle, primarily through her association with Molly Somerville. The text describes Sylvia’s oscillation between the intellectual rigor of her home life and the frivolous, material-focused life of her wealthy peers. She begins to adopt the mannerisms of the elite, distancing herself from the "freaks" and "grinds."

The Arrival of Aunt Victoria

The narrative trajectory shifts with the arrival of Aunt Victoria, Professor Marshall's estranged, wealthy sister-in-law (widow of his brother). Victoria arrives in La Chance with an aura of cosmopolitan glamour, expensive clothes, and a "maid." The text records the immediate impact on Sylvia, who sees in Victoria the embodiment of her aspirations.

Victoria invites Sylvia to accompany her for the summer to Lydford, a fashionable resort in the East. Mrs. Marshall opposes the trip initially but eventually concedes. Sylvia departs, leaving La Chance behind for a world of luxury.

"She was going to Lydford! She was going to live with Aunt Victoria! She was going to see the world! She was going to be happy!"

Life in Lydford and New York

In Lydford, the narrative documents Sylvia’s immersion in high society. She is outfitted in expensive clothing provided by Victoria. The text details the daily routine: late breakfasts, tennis, tea, dinners, and constant social maneuvering. Sylvia is successful in this environment; her beauty and "freshness" attract attention.

She meets Felix Morrison, a sophisticated, wealthy architect and art critic. The text records their dialogues, which center on aesthetics, the "art of living," and the rejection of the "sordid" aspects of existence. Morrison courts Sylvia, and she falls in love with him and the life he represents.

"He made her feel that she was of the elect... that the world was made for the appreciation of the few who had eyes to see."

The narrative introduces a conflict when Sylvia begins to perceive the underlying mechanics of this society. She observes the treatment of servants and the rigidity of social exclusion. A specific scene involves a young woman who is ostracized for a minor social infraction. Sylvia witnesses the cruelty of the "inner circle," including Morrison’s passive acceptance of these norms.

The Paris Interval and the Turning Point

Sylvia accompanies Aunt Victoria to Paris. The text describes the height of their luxury living. However, the narrative records a series of observations that destabilize Sylvia’s view of Victoria. She notices the presence of older men who seem to sustain Victoria’s lifestyle in exchange for her company, though the text keeps the exact nature of these relationships socially ambiguous but morally suggestive to Sylvia.

The breaking point occurs when Sylvia overhears a conversation regarding Victoria’s dependency on a wealthy patron, implying that Victoria is a "kept woman" or at least financially compromised in a way that contradicts the Marshall family's code of independence.

"It was not the words, but the tone... a tone of easy, tolerant, cynical familiarity that stripped the veil from her eyes."

Simultaneously, Sylvia receives a letter from home detailing a crisis: Judith has contracted typhoid fever, and the family is under strain. The contrast between the "useless" beauty of her current life and the "reality" of her family’s struggle in La Chance becomes untenable. Sylvia decides to leave. She confronts Victoria, not with a dramatic denunciation, but with a firm decision to return to her "own people."

"I am going home," said Sylvia. "I belong there."

Return to La Chance

Sylvia returns to find Judith recovering. The text documents Sylvia’s readjustment to the "plain living" of the Marshall household. She enters a period of despondency, feeling she has lost the "bright world," but also feeling a new respect for her parents' integrity.

The Courtship of Felix Morrison

Felix Morrison follows Sylvia to La Chance. The narrative records his attempt to integrate into the Marshall’s world to win Sylvia. He is charming and respectful of Professor Marshall, though the text notes the fundamental incompatibility between his aestheticism and the Marshalls' rugged idealism.

Sylvia is tempted again. Morrison offers her a return to the life she desires, sanitized by marriage. They become engaged. The narrative describes Sylvia’s internal rationalization: she believes she can have Morrison’s wealth without losing her soul.

The Intrusion of Reality

The engagement is disrupted by the appearance of Austin Page. Page is a former student of Professor Marshall, now a man who has devoted his life to social reform and labor rights, specifically working in coal mines. He is described as physically unimpressive but intensely vital.

The text records interactions between Morrison and Page. Morrison views Page as a fanatic; Page views Morrison as irrelevant. Sylvia observes the two men. The narrative brings the conflict to a head during a discussion about a labor strike. Morrison advocates for order and the protection of culture; Page advocates for justice and the relief of suffering.

"Mr. Page spoke of the miners as if they were human beings... Mr. Morrison spoke of them as if they were a dangerous natural force to be controlled."

Sylvia realizes that Morrison’s refinement depends on the labor of the very people he dismisses. She begins to see his "art of living" as parasitic. The text documents a specific scene where Sylvia questions Morrison about his income sources, mirroring her earlier realization about Aunt Victoria. Morrison’s vague and defensive answers confirm her fears.

The Break-Up

Sylvia breaks the engagement with Morrison. The text records the dialogue where she explains that she cannot live his life.

"I can't do it, Felix. I can't breathe in that air. It is too thin. I should starve."

Morrison departs. Sylvia is left alone in La Chance, having rejected the wealth she spent her youth coveting.

C. Finale (Conclusion)
The narrative moves to the final phase of Sylvia’s development. She enrolls in state reform work, following a path similar to Austin Page. The text documents her transition from a passive observer of social ills to an active participant in their remediation.

The Crisis of Professor Marshall

A subplot concludes regarding Professor Marshall. He is threatened with dismissal from the university due to his unorthodox views and his defense of academic freedom. The text records the family’s solidarity. Mrs. Marshall remains unshakeable. Sylvia, now fully aligned with her family’s values, supports her father. The dismissal is averted, but the event serves to cement the family's moral victory.

Austin Page and the Final Choice

Austin Page returns to La Chance. He and Sylvia work together on local social issues. The narrative records the lack of traditional "romance" in their courtship; instead, it is built on shared work and mutual respect.

Sylvia struggles with the fear that a life with Page will be "drab" and "joyless," the very things she feared as a child. However, the text documents a shift in her perception. She begins to find beauty in the "reality" of the work.

The climax of the finale occurs when Page is injured or falls ill (the text varies in editions, often a result of his labor work). Sylvia rushes to him. The narrative records her realization that her love for him is grounded in his connection to the "struggling world."

The Proposal and Acceptance

The final scenes take place in the Marshall garden, bringing the narrative full circle to the setting of the introduction. Austin Page proposes to Sylvia, not with promises of ease, but with a promise of shared burden.

"I have nothing to offer you," said Austin Page, "but a share in the hardest work there is."

Sylvia accepts him. She explicitly acknowledges that she is choosing the "bent twig" of her upbringing—the values instilled by her parents—over the artificial straightening offered by society.

"I am not afraid," said Sylvia. "I want to live. I want to be real."

Final Narrative State

The book concludes with Sylvia and Austin preparing for their life together. The text describes Mrs. Marshall watching them, satisfied that the "twig" has finally set in the right direction. The final image is one of continuity and endurance, with Sylvia fully inhabiting the identity she once fought against.

"She saw the road before her, rough and steep, but she knew that she had the strength to climb it, for she was her father's daughter, and her mother's child."

(Note: The narrative ends with the certainty of the marriage and the alignment of values, documenting the completed arc from rebellion to acceptance of the Marshall ethos.)
