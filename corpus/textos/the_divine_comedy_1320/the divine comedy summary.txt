1. Narrator & Characters (Tag-Based, Factual)

Narrator Metadata

    Narrator Voice: First-person participant (The Pilgrim)

    Narrator Perspective: Retrospective experiential

    Narrator Type: Autodiegetic

    Knowledge Scope: Limited to immediate sensory perception and external instruction; expands only via direct revelation or interlocutor explanation

    Narrative Mediation: Direct address to the reader; intermittent invocations to Muses, High Fantasy, and God

2. Comprehensive Plot Summary (Written Order Only)
A. Introduction

The Dark Wood and the Three Beasts

The narrative record begins midway through the narrator’s life. The narrator, identifying himself as a traveler, finds himself physically located within a dark, savage forest, having lost the direct path. The environment is described as dense and harsh, inducing fear that mirrors the bitterness of death. He cannot account for how he entered the wood, citing a state of sleepiness at the moment he abandoned the true way.

    "Midway upon the journey of our life / I found myself within a forest dark, / For the straightforward pathway had been lost."

The traveler arrives at the foot of a hill where the valley ends. Looking upward, he observes the hill's shoulders clothed in the rays of the planet that leads men straight on every road. This sight quiets the fear in his heart. He attempts to ascend the slope.

His progress is physically blocked by three beasts appearing in sequence:

    A leopard, light and swift, with a spotted coat, which impedes his path so persistently he turns often to retreat.

    A lion, approaching with head held high and rabid hunger.

    A she-wolf, appearing laden with all cravings in her leanness, causing such heaviness of spirit that the traveler loses hope of the height.

The Appearance of Virgil

Driven back into the lowlands where the sun is silent, the traveler perceives a figure seemingly faint from long silence. He cries out for pity. The figure identifies himself not as a living man, but as one who was formerly a man, a Lombard by birth, who lived under Augustus and wrote of the just son of Anchises who came from Troy.

The traveler recognizes the figure as Virgil, his "master and my author." Virgil states that the traveler must take "another road" to escape the savage place, citing the she-wolf’s nature to kill all who pass until the "Greyhound" comes to slay her.

The Mandate

Virgil outlines the itinerary: first, they will hear the cries of despair in an eternal place; second, they will see those who are content in fire because they hope to come eventually to the blessed; third, if the traveler wishes to ascend to the blessed, a soul worthier than Virgil will guide him, as Virgil is forbidden from the city of the Emperor on high.

The traveler expresses doubt regarding his worthiness, comparing himself unfavorably to Aeneas and Paul: "I am not Aeneas, I am not Paul."

Virgil rebukes this cowardice, explaining his motivation. He was in Limbo when a lady, blessed and beautiful (Beatrice), descended to ask him to assist the traveler. She stated she was moved by love. Virgil reports that Beatrice was urged by Saint Lucia, who was in turn instructed by a "Gentle Lady" in Heaven (Mary) who broke the stern judgment against the traveler.

Fortified by this knowledge, the traveler accepts the mission.

    "On, then! For a single will is in us both: / Thou leader, and thou lord, and master thou."

They move toward the deep and savage way.
B. The Body (Rising Action)

INFERNO: The Descent

The Gate and the Vestibule
They arrive at a gate bearing the inscription concluding: "ABANDON ALL HOPE, YE WHO ENTER HERE." The traveler hears sighs, laments, and loud wailings resounding through the starless air. Virgil identifies these as the sorrowful souls who lived without disgrace and without praise, mixed with the angels who were neither rebellious nor faithful to God, but stood apart. These souls, the Neutrals, chase a banner that never pauses, while stung by gadflies and wasps; worms at their feet consume their blood and tears.

The River Acheron
They reach the shore of Acheron. Charon, an old man with white hair and wheels of fire around his eyes, refuses passage to the living traveler. Virgil commands him:

    "Charon, bite back thy spleen: / This has been willed where what is willed must be, / And ask no more."

The earth trembles, wind flashes red light, and the traveler loses consciousness.

Circle I: Limbo
He awakens on the other side. They enter the First Circle, Limbo. No wailing is heard, only sighs causing the air to tremble. Virgil explains these are the unbaptized and those who lived before Christianity; they do not suffer physical torment but exist in hope without fulfillment. The traveler meets the four great poets—Homer, Horace, Ovid, and Lucan—who welcome him as the sixth of their company. They view a noble castle containing heroic figures of antiquity (Hector, Aeneas, Caesar, Aristotle, Plato).

Circle II: The Lustful
They descend to a smaller circumference with greater pain. Minos, the judge, examines offenses by coiling his tail. He warns the traveler against entering. In the dark wind, souls are driven violently by a storm. The traveler speaks to two souls drifting together: Paolo and Francesca. Francesca explains that love seized them while reading of Lancelot.

    "A Galeotto was the book and he who wrote it: / That day no farther did we read therein."

The traveler faints out of pity.

Circle III: The Gluttonous
Recovering, he finds himself under heavy, cold, accursed rain. Cerberus, a three-headed dog, barks over the submerged souls. Virgil throws earth into its mouths. Ciacco, a Florentine, rises from the mud, predicts bloodshed in Florence (White and Black Guelphs), and asks to be remembered.

Circle IV: The Avaricious and Prodigal
Plutus guards this circle, collapsing at Virgil’s word. Two groups of souls push heavy weights with their chests in a circle, clashing and yelling "Why do you hoard?" and "Why do you squander?" Virgil explains the nature of Fortune as an ordained minister transferring goods between nations.

Circle V: The Wrathful and Sullen
They descend to the marsh of the Styx. Muddy figures strike each other in the slime. Beneath the water, the Sullen gurgle a hymn of their previous gloomy nature. Phlegyas ferries them across the Styx. Filippo Argenti attempts to capsize the boat; the traveler wishes to see him soused in the broth, a wish Virgil praises.

The City of Dis
They approach the iron walls of the City of Dis. Fallen angels deny them entry, slamming the gates. The Furies appear on a tower, calling for Medusa to turn the traveler to stone. Virgil covers the traveler’s eyes. A Heavenly Messenger arrives, crossing the Styx dry-shod, and opens the gate with a wand.

Circle VI: The Heretics
Inside the walls, the traveler sees an open plain filled with flaming tombs. Farinata degli Uberti, a Ghibelline leader, rises from a tomb. He discusses Florentine lineage with the traveler. Cavalcante dei Cavalcanti interrupts, asking for his son Guido. Farinata predicts the traveler’s exile.

Circle VII: The Violent

    Round 1 (Violence against Neighbors): They descend a landslide. The Minotaur bites himself in rage. They cross the river of boiling blood, the Phlegethon. Centaurs led by Chiron patrol the banks. Nessus carries the traveler across, pointing out tyrants submerged in blood.

    Round 2 (Violence against Self): They enter a pathless wood of gnarled, thorny trees. The traveler snaps a twig, and the trunk cries out, "Why do you tear me?" This is Pier delle Vigne. He explains that suicides become seeds, growing into trees pecked by Harpies.

    Round 3 (Violence against God, Nature, Art): A plain of burning sand under a rain of fire. Capaneus lies supine, scorning the rain. The traveler meets his old teacher, Brunetto Latini, who walks under the fire. Brunetto prophesies the traveler's future success and warns against the Fiesolan people.

    "If thou follow but thy star, / Thou canst not miss at last a glorious haven."

The Descent on Geryon
Virgil throws a cord into the abyss. Geryon, a monster with a benign face and a scorpion’s tail, rises. The traveler mounts Geryon’s shoulders for the flight down to the Eighth Circle.

Circle VIII: Malebolge (Fraud)
Divided into ten concentric ditches (bolge).

    Panderers and Seducers: Whipped by horned demons. Jason is observed.

    Flatterers: Submerged in excrement.

    Simonists: Buried upside down with feet on fire. The traveler speaks to Pope Nicholas III, who mistakes him for Boniface VIII. The traveler rebukes the Pope for commercializing the church.

    Diviners: Heads twisted backward, walking backward crying tears down their buttocks.

    Barrators: Submerged in boiling pitch guarded by Malebranche demons. Malacoda (demon leader) gives false directions about a bridge.

    Hypocrites: Wearing leaden cloaks gilded on the outside. Caiaphas is crucified on the ground, trampled by the others.

    Thieves: Pursued by snakes. Vanni Fucci makes an obscene gesture (figs) at God. Snakes bind limbs; a lizard merges with a man, exchanging forms in a grotesque metamorphosis.

    Fraudulent Counselors: Concealed within flames. The traveler sees a double-tipped flame containing Ulysses and Diomedes. Ulysses recounts his final voyage past the Pillars of Hercules to the "world without people," ending in a shipwreck.

    "Consider ye the seed from which ye sprang; / Ye were not made to live like unto brutes, / But for pursuit of virtue and of knowledge."

    Sowers of Scandal and Schism: Mutilated by a demon with a sword. Mahomet is split from chin to anus. Bertran de Born carries his severed head by the hair like a lantern.

    Falsifiers: Afflicted with diseases (scabs, fever, dropsy). Master Adam (counterfeiter) and Sinon (Greek spy) exchange blows and insults.

Circle IX: Cocytus (Treachery)
Giants (Nimrod, Ephialtes, Antaeus) stand in a well. Antaeus lowers the poets onto the frozen lake of Cocytus.

    Caina (Kin): Souls frozen head and neck.

    Antenora (Country): The traveler kicks a face (Bocca degli Abati). He sees one sinner gnawing the skull of another. Count Ugolino lifts his mouth from Archbishop Ruggieri’s skull to tell how he was imprisoned with his sons and starved, implying cannibalism of their corpses.

    Ptolomea (Guests): Tears freeze in eye sockets. Fra Alberigo explains that souls can fall here before death, their bodies possessed by demons on earth.

    Judecca (Masters): Souls fully submerged.

The Center of Hell
They reach Satan (Dis). He is a giant tri-faced creature (red, yellow-white, black) flapping bat wings that freeze the lake. In his three mouths, he chews Judas Iscariot, Brutus, and Cassius.
Virgil carries the traveler, climbing down Satan’s shaggy hide. They pass the hip (center of gravity) and climb up, exiting through a cavern to see the stars.

PURGATORIO: The Ascent

The Shore
Emerging at dawn on the island of Purgatory. They meet Cato of Utica, the guardian. Virgil instructs the traveler to gird himself with a smooth rush and wash his face. They see a boat piloted by an angel bringing souls singing In exitu Israel de Aegypto. The traveler recognizes Casella, a musician, who sings a poem by Dante. Cato chides them for lingering.

Ante-Purgatory

    The Excommunicate: Souls who delayed repentance wander at the base. Manfred asks the traveler to tell his daughter he is saved.

    The Late Repentant: Belacqua sits lazily in the shade, explaining he must wait a lifetime before entering.

    The Valley of Rulers: Sordello embraces Virgil upon hearing "Mantua." They watch the sun set. Two angels drive away a serpent.

The Gate of Purgatory
The traveler dreams of an eagle (Lucia) carrying him up. He wakes at the Gate. An angel with a sword sits on three steps (white marble, dark cracked stone, porphyry). The angel inscribes seven "P"s (Peccatum) on the traveler’s forehead and opens the door with silver and gold keys.

Terrace 1: Pride
Souls carry massive stones, forcing them to bow. The pavement displays sculptures of humility (Annunciation, David) and pride (Tower of Babel). The traveler walks bent over. He meets Omberto Aldobrandeschi and Oderisi da Gubbio (illuminator). Oderisi speaks on the fleeting nature of artistic fame.
As they exit, an angel erases one "P".

Terrace 2: Envy
Souls wear gray cloaks and have their eyelids sewn shut with iron wire. They lean on each other. Sapia of Siena speaks. The traveler admits he fears the punishment of pride more than envy.

Terrace 3: Wrath
Thick black smoke blinds them. Souls pray Agnus Dei. Marco Lombardo explains that the world is blind because bad government, not stellar influence, corrupts man. He asserts the need for separation of Church and State (two suns).

Terrace 4: Sloth
Souls run constantly, shouting examples of zeal and sloth. Virgil explains the structure of Purgatory based on love (perverted, defective, or excessive).

Terrace 5: Avarice and Prodigality
Souls lie face down, bound hand and foot, weeping. Pope Adrian V refuses the traveler's reverence. Suddenly, the mountain trembles violently. The soul of Statius appears, explaining the quake occurred because he felt the will to rise to Heaven, having completed his penance. Statius reveals he was a closet Christian and a poet inspired by the Aeneid. He attempts to embrace Virgil's feet.

Terrace 6: Gluttony
Erysichthon and Mary of Brabant are cited. Souls are emaciated, eyes sunk in sockets. They pass apple trees that recede or cannot be reached. Forese Donati converses with the traveler. Bonagiunta da Lucca identifies the traveler as the creator of the "Sweet New Style" (Dolce Stil Novo).

Terrace 7: Lust
Two groups of souls walk through a wall of flame, exchanging kisses of greeting. They shout examples of chastity and lust. Guido Guinizzelli and Arnaut Daniel (speaking in Provençal) are present.
The angel tells the traveler he must pass through the fire. He hesitates until Virgil reminds him Beatrice is on the other side. He enters the heat.

The Earthly Paradise
They sleep on the steps. Virgil declares the traveler’s will is now "free, upright, and whole," and crowns and miters him over himself.
They enter a divine forest. Matelda, a lady singing and gathering flowers, explains the wind and water (Lethe and Eunoe) of the place.
A grand procession approaches: seven candlesticks, twenty-four elders, four beasts, and a chariot drawn by a Griffin (Christ).

Beatrice's Arrival
A lady appears on the chariot in a cloud of flowers, wearing a white veil, green cape, and red dress. The traveler turns to Virgil for comfort ("Not a drachm of blood remains in me that does not tremble"), but Virgil is gone.
Beatrice calls him by name—the only time his name appears in the text.

    "Dante, because Virgil leaves thee, / Weep not, weep not thou yet."

She rebukes him sternly for straying from the path after her death. He confesses, weeping. He is immersed in the River Lethe by Matelda to erase memories of sin. He sees the Griffin reflect in Beatrice’s eyes. He is led to the River Eunoe to restore memories of good deeds.

    "From the most holy water I returned / Regenerate, in the manner of new trees / That are renewed with a new foliage, / Pure and disposed to mount unto the stars."

PARADISO: The Ascension

The Ascent
The traveler and Beatrice gaze at the sun. They are transhumanized and rise toward the heavens. Beatrice explains the order of the universe where all things have a natural tendency toward their source.

Sphere 1: The Moon (The Inconstant)
They enter the pearl-like substance of the moon. Souls appear as faint reflections. Piccarda Donati explains she was forced from her convent. She clarifies that souls here are perfectly content with their station because their will is entirely aligned with God’s will.

    "In His will is our peace."

Sphere 2: Mercury (The Ambitious)
Souls appear as joyful lights. Emperor Justinian speaks at length on the history of the Roman Eagle and divine justice. He explains that the ambitious did good deeds for fame, limiting their capacity for higher love.

Sphere 3: Venus (The Lovers)
The planet spins with the Seraphim. Charles Martel discusses heredity and society. Cunizza da Romano and Folquet de Marseille are present. Rahab is pointed out as the first soul assumed to this sphere.

Sphere 4: The Sun (The Wise)
A crown of twelve glowing lights encircles the travelers. St. Thomas Aquinas (Dominican) praises St. Francis of Assisi. A second circle forms; St. Bonaventure (Franciscan) praises St. Dominic. They criticize the corruption in their own respective orders.

Sphere 5: Mars (The Warriors of Faith)
Souls form a gigantic Greek Cross of light, with Christ flashing at the center. Cacciaguida, the traveler’s ancestor, descends to the foot of the cross. He describes the ancient, pure Florence and predicts the traveler’s coming exile and his duty to write the truth without fear.

    "This cry of thine shall do as doth the wind, / Which smiteth most the loftiest summits."

Sphere 6: Jupiter (The Just)
Souls spell out DILIGITE JUSTITIAM QUI JUDICATIS TERRAM. The final 'M' transforms into the shape of an Eagle. The Eagle speaks as a single voice for the many souls, discussing divine justice and the salvation of pagans (Trajan and Ripheus are present in the Eagle’s eye).

Sphere 7: Saturn (The Contemplatives)
A golden ladder stretches up out of sight. No music plays here to spare the traveler’s senses. St. Peter Damian speaks on predestination. St. Benedict condemns the corruption of monasteries.

Sphere 8: The Fixed Stars
The traveler looks back down at the "threshing floor" of Earth, seeing it as small and insignificant.
The Triumph of Christ appears: a sun illuminating thousands of lamps. Mary is crowned by the Angel Gabriel (as a sapphire light) and ascends.
The traveler undergoes an examination on the three theological virtues:

    Faith: Examined by St. Peter.

    Hope: Examined by St. James.

    Charity: Examined by St. John.
    Adam appears and answers questions about the language he spoke and the length of his time in Eden.

Sphere 9: The Primum Mobile
They enter the Crystalline Sphere. The traveler sees a point of intense light surrounded by nine rings of fire (the Angelic Orders). Beatrice explains the inversion: in the physical world, the largest sphere is most divine; here, the smallest ring (closest to God) is fastest and most divine.
C. Finale (Conclusion)

The Empyrean
They issue forth from the largest body into the heaven of pure light.

    "Light intellectual full-laden with love, / Love of true good, full-laden with gladness, / Gladness that transcendeth every sweetness."

The traveler’s sight is strengthened. He sees a river of light, with sparks (angels) entering and leaving flowers (souls). The river transforms into a circular sea of light: the Celestial Rose. The souls of the blessed are seated on the petals.

Beatrice’s Departure
The traveler turns to ask Beatrice a question but finds an elder man in her place. This is Saint Bernard. He points to Beatrice, who has returned to her throne in the third rank from the highest. The traveler prays his thanks to her for his freedom; she smiles down at him one last time, then turns to the eternal fountain.

The Vision
Saint Bernard identifies the Queen of Heaven, Mary. He guides the traveler’s gaze through the hierarchy of the Rose (Eve, Rachel, Sarah, etc.). Bernard offers a prayer to the Virgin Mary, asking her to allow the traveler to behold the Primal Source without his mortality failing him.

    "Virgin Mother, daughter of thy Son, / Humble and high beyond all other creature."

Mary looks upon God, then the traveler looks upward.

The Ultimate Vision
The traveler’s vision penetrates the Infinite Good. He sees three things:

    The Universal Form: He sees the scattered leaves of the universe bound by love in one volume.

    The Trinity: He sees three circles of three distinct colors but equal dimension. One seems reflected by the other as rainbow by rainbow (Son from Father), and the third is fire breathed equally from both (Holy Spirit).

    The Incarnation: Within the circle painted in its own color, he perceives the human effigy.

He attempts to understand how the image fits the circle (how the human unites with the divine). He compares himself to a geometer trying to square the circle. His own wings are insufficient for this flight.

The End of the Will
A flash of understanding strikes his mind, fulfilling his wish. The archival record concludes with the cessation of the traveler's own power of fantasy, replaced by the movement of his desire and will by God.

    "But already my desire and my will were being turned / Like a wheel that is evenly moved / By the Love that moves the sun and the other stars."
