2. Comprehensive Plot Summary
A. Introduction
The Prophecy of the Middle Station

The narrative opens in the year 1632, in the city of York. Robinson Crusoe introduces himself as the third son of a family of good estate. His father, a grave and ancient man originally from Bremen, calls Robinson into his chamber to discuss his future. Robinson has expressed a strong inclination to go to sea, a desire his father vehemently opposes.

The text documents the specific arguments Crusoe's father employs. He identifies Robinson’s station as the "middle state," or the "upper station of low life." He argues that this position is the most calculated for human happiness, free from the miseries of the poor and the anxieties of the rich. He warns Robinson that going to sea is a desperate action suitable only for men of desperate fortunes or superior ambition. He prophesies that if Robinson persists in this "wandering inclination," God will not bless him.

"He told me it was for men of desperate fortunes on one hand, or of aspiring, superior fortunes on the other, who went abroad upon adventures... that mine was the middle state, or what might be called the upper station of low life, which he had found by long experience was the best state in the world."

The father’s exhortation concludes with tears. He warns that if Robinson leaves, he will have leisure to repent when there is no one to deliver him. Robinson is initially moved and resolves to stay home, but this resolution wears off within a few weeks. Without his parents' blessing, he departs for London on September 1, 1651, aboard a ship from Hull.

The First Voyage and Shipwreck

Almost immediately, the ship encounters a storm in the Yarmouth Roads. Robinson is terrified and falls into a state of deep repentance, vowing to return to his father if he survives. However, as the weather clears, his companion mocks his fear, calling it a "cap-full of wind," and Robinson drinks away his penitence with punch.

A second, more violent storm strikes. The ship founders and sinks. Robinson and the crew are rescued by a longboat from a nearby light collier. They land near Cromer and walk to Yarmouth. The master of the ship, upon hearing Robinson’s story, tells him he should never go to sea again, as he is clearly a visible marker of bad luck.

"Young man," says he, "you ought never to go to sea any more; you ought to take this for a plain and visible token that you are not to be a seafaring man."

Robinson ignores this warning. Ashamed to return home to the ridicule of his neighbors, he travels to London.

Slavery and Escape from Sallee

In London, Robinson befriends a ship's master and embarks on a successful trading voyage to Guinea. He returns with gold dust and a desire to continue as a "Guinea trader." However, on his next voyage, the ship is chased and captured by a Turkish rover from Sallee. Robinson is taken prisoner and made a slave to the captain of the pirate vessel.

He spends two years in slavery, handling the garden and the household of his master. He constantly meditates on escape. The opportunity arises when his master sends him out fishing in a longboat with a young Moor named Ismael and a boy named Xury. Robinson tricks them into loading the boat with extra provisions and guns.

Once at sea, Robinson pushes Ismael overboard, threatening to shoot him if he attempts to return to the boat. He turns to the boy, Xury, and demands his loyalty.

"I turned to the boy, whom they called Xury, and said to him, 'Xury, if you will be faithful to me I will make you a great man; but if you will not stroke your face to be true to me, that is, swear by Mahomet and his father's beard, I must throw you into the sea too.' The boy smiled in my face, and spoke so innocently, that I could not mistrust him; and swore to be faithful to me, and go all over the world with me."

The Brazil Plantation and the Fatal Proposal

Robinson and Xury sail south along the African coast, fearing the wild beasts on shore and the savages. They kill a lion and preserve its skin. Eventually, they are sighted by a Portuguese ship bound for Brazil. The captain takes them aboard. He acts generously, buying Robinson’s boat and offering to pay for Xury. Robinson is reluctant to sell the boy who helped him escape, but agrees when the captain promises to set Xury free in ten years if he turns Christian.

In Brazil, Robinson becomes a planter. He purchases land and, over four years, begins to prosper, growing tobacco and sugar. However, he becomes dissatisfied with the slow road to wealth. He views his life in Brazil as exactly the "middle station" his father had advised him to accept in England, which he had fled to avoid.

"I was coming into the very middle station, or upper degree of low life, which my father advised me to before; and which, if I resolved to go on with, I might as well have stayed at home."

His fellow planters, hearing his stories of the Guinea coast, propose a secret voyage. They want Robinson to act as supercargo to purchase slaves directly from Africa, which would be cheaper than buying them through public channels. Robinson, unable to resist his "propensity of nature," agrees. He makes a will, leaving his plantation in the care of the Portuguese captain, and embarks on September 1, 1659—the anniversary of his original departure from Hull.

B. The Body (Rising Action)
The Shipwreck on the Island

The ship sails north, crossing the equator. They are caught in a violent hurricane that drives them westward, far off course. The crew spots land, but the ship strikes a sandbank. The waves break over the vessel with such force that the men abandon ship in a small boat. The boat is swamped by a "mountain-like" wave, and all are drowned except Robinson Crusoe.

He is dashed against a rock but manages to cling to it and scramble ashore. He is the sole survivor. He spends the first night in a tree, terrified of wild beasts. The next morning, he realizes the ship has been driven closer to the shore by the tide.

"I was now landed, and safe on shore, and began to look up and thank God that my life was saved, in a case wherein there was some minutes before scarce any room to hope."

Establishing the Habitation

Crusoe swims to the wreck. He constructs a raft from spare yards and masts to transport goods. He systematically strips the ship, prioritizing useful items: bread, rice, Dutch cheeses, rum, carpenter's tools, muskets, pistols, and powder. He also finds "two or three bags full of money," which he initially scorns as "drug," but ultimately decides to take with him.

He finds a flat plateau on the side of a hill to build his fortress. He pitches a tent and surrounds it with a semi-circle of strong stakes, driven deep into the ground. He uses a cable from the ship to reinforce the fence. He does not create a door but uses a ladder to go over the top, pulling it in after him for security. He expands the natural cave behind the tent to serve as a cellar or "kitchen."

Crusoe erects a large post on the shore to mark time, cutting a notch for every day.

"I came on shore here on the 30th of September 1659."

Spiritual Awakening and Survival

The narrative transitions into a journal format for a period. Crusoe records his daily labors. He struggles to make a shovel from "iron-wood" (lignum vitae) and attempts to mold earthen pots. He discovers a few stalks of barley and rice growing near his fortification. Initially, he attributes this to a miracle, but later recalls shaking out a bag of chicken feed in that spot.

He falls ill with a violent ague (fever). During a fever dream, he sees a man descend from a black cloud with a spear, threatening to kill him for not repenting. This terrifies him. He realizes he has lived without any thought of God. He searches the sea-chest and finds a Bible and tobacco. He takes the tobacco (steeped in rum) for his body and the Bible for his soul. He opens the book casually and reads:

"Call on me in the day of trouble, and I will deliver thee, and thou shalt glorify me."

This verse begins his religious conversion. He begins to pray and to observe the Sabbath by cutting a larger notch in his post.

Domesticating the Island

Crusoe explores the island. He finds a valley with melons, grapes, and cocoa trees. He builds a "bower" or country seat in this fertile area but maintains his fortress on the coast. He catches a young parrot (Poll) and teaches it to speak. He begins to capture and tame goats, eventually building enclosures to keep a flock for meat and milk.

He spends months trying to make a pot that will hold water and withstand fire. After many failures, he succeeds in firing his earthenware. He also details the tedious process of making bread: growing the corn without a plough, grinding it without a mill, and baking it without an oven (using earthen tiles).

He attempts to build a canoe from a massive cedar tree. He spends months hewing it, only to realize it is too heavy to move to the water. This failure teaches him the folly of planning without calculating the necessary labor. He later builds a smaller canoe (periagua) and successfully navigates it, though he is nearly swept out to sea by a current.

"I found that the want of two things, viz. a horse and a guide, was such, that I had no possibility of practicing this with success; and therefore I gave this attempt over."

The Footprint and the Cannibals

After fifteen years on the island, Crusoe makes a terrifying discovery. He sees the print of a man's naked foot on the sand. He is seized with paranoia. He retreats to his fortress, now calling it his "castle," and does not stir for three days. He imagines it might be the Devil, then concludes it must be savages from the mainland.

"It was very very lately that I was so cast down and sunk in my spirits... and now I was made so tremble with the very apprehensions of seeing a man, and was ready to sink into the ground at but the shadow or silent appearance of a man's having set his foot in the island."

He reinforces his outer wall with a second layer of stakes and plants a dense grove of trees to conceal his habitation. Two years later, he discovers the shore spread with skulls, hands, and other human bones, along with the remains of a fire. He realizes the island is a visiting place for cannibals.

He becomes consumed with thoughts of destroying them. He positions muskets and spends months watching for their return. However, he eventually reasons that he has no divine authority to judge or execute them, as they have done him no specific harm. He resolves to remain concealed.

The Arrival of Friday

In his twenty-third year, Crusoe spots a group of savages on the shore. He watches through his telescope as they drag two prisoners from their canoes. One is knocked down and butchered; the other, seeing a chance, sprints away toward Crusoe’s concealment. He is pursued by two savages.

Crusoe decides this is his time to acquire a servant or companion. He intercepts the pursuers, knocking one down with his gun butt and shooting the other. The escaping prisoner is terrified by the noise of the gun. Crusoe beckons him. The man approaches, kneels, and places Crusoe’s foot on his head in a sign of absolute submission.

Crusoe names him "Friday" in honor of the day he was saved. He teaches Friday to speak English, to eat goat instead of human flesh, and to wear clothes.

"I likewise taught him to say Master; and then let him know that was to be my name."

Friday's Conversion and the Plan to Depart

Crusoe instructs Friday in the knowledge of the true God. They discuss the nature of the Devil. Friday asks a difficult theological question: if God is so strong, why does he not kill the Devil? Crusoe struggles to answer but explains the concept of repentance and judgment.

Friday informs Crusoe that there are white men ("bearded men") living with his nation on the mainland. Crusoe deduces these are the survivors of a Spanish ship he had seen wrecked some years prior. They build a new, large canoe together. Crusoe intends to sail to the mainland to join the Spaniards.

The Rescue of the Spaniard

Before they can leave, the cannibals return with three prisoners. Crusoe and Friday attack them to save the victims. They kill most of the savages with gunfire. One of the prisoners turns out to be a Spaniard. Another prisoner, found bound in a boat, is discovered to be Friday’s father.

The reunion between Friday and his father is emotional. The text describes Friday kissing, embracing, and crying over his father.

"He sat down by him, held the old man's head close to his bosom, and chafed his arms and ankles, which were stiff and numbed with the binding."

The Spaniard explains that there are sixteen of his countrymen living in misery among the savages. Crusoe agrees to help them, but first, they must expand their agriculture to feed such a large group. After a season of farming, Crusoe sends the Spaniard and Friday's father back to the mainland in the canoe to fetch the others.

C. Finale (Conclusion)
The English Mutineers

While waiting for the Spaniard's return, an English ship appears. A longboat comes ashore near Crusoe’s dwelling. Through his perspective glass, Crusoe observes that the crew has brought three prisoners with them. The prisoners are left on the beach while the mutineers wander the woods and eventually fall asleep due to alcohol.

Crusoe approaches the prisoners. He is heavily armed and dressed in his goat-skin clothes, looking formidable. He speaks to them, startling them. The leader identifies himself as the Captain of the ship, overthrown by a mutiny led by his boatswain.

Crusoe offers a contract: he will save them on two conditions. First, they must obey his authority while on the island. Second, if they recover the ship, they must carry him and his man Friday to England passage-free. The Captain agrees, calling Crusoe a man sent from heaven.

"Am I talking to God or man! Is it a real man or an angel!"

The Retaking of the Ship

Crusoe supplies the Captain and his two loyal men with arms. They kill the ringleaders of the mutiny when they awake. The rest of the longboat crew surrenders. Crusoe keeps them separated, using the fear of his "governor" persona (he pretends to have a garrison of fifty men) to keep them compliant.

A second boat is sent from the ship to investigate the delay of the first. Crusoe organizes an ambush. He has Friday and the Captain's mate call out to the men in the boat to lure them into the interior of the island. In the darkness, Crusoe’s party surrounds and captures them. The Captain kills the rebel boatswain during the skirmish.

Crusoe intimidates the remaining prisoners, claiming the "Governor" intends to execute them all in England, but might pardon them if they help retake the ship. They agree. The Captain leads the assault on the ship by night. The surprise is complete; the pirate captain is shot, and the ship is secured.

The English Captain returns to the shore and presents the ship to Crusoe.

"My dear friend and deliverer,' says he, 'there's your ship, for she is all yours, and so are we, and all that belong to her."

The Fate of the Mutineers

Crusoe dresses in the Captain’s clothes, transforming back into a civilized figure. He decides the fate of the surviving mutineers. Rather than taking them to England to be hanged, he offers them the choice to remain on the island. They accept gratefully.

Crusoe provides them with instructions on how to survive. He leaves them his firearms, his tools, and his crops. He tells them about the Spaniards who are expected to return soon and leaves a letter for them.

Departure and Return to England

Crusoe leaves the island on December 19, 1686, having lived there for twenty-eight years, two months, and nineteen days. He arrives in England in June 1687. He finds that his parents are dead, and his family is dispersed. He is a stranger in his own land.

He travels to Lisbon to inquire about his estate in Brazil. He meets the Portuguese captain who saved him years ago. The captain reveals that Crusoe’s investments have been incredibly profitable. The trustees have been managing the plantation and holding the wealth for him. Crusoe finds himself suddenly a master of over five thousand pounds sterling in ready money, with a large estate in Brazil.

"I was now master, all on a sudden, of above £5000 sterling in money, and had an estate, as I might well call it, in the Brazil, of above £1000 a year, as sure as an estate of lands in England."

The Journey Over the Pyrenees

Crusoe decides to return to England by land to avoid the sea, which he still distrusts. He travels with Friday and a group of merchants through the Pyrenees mountains. The text documents a specific encounter with a pack of ravenous wolves in the snow. Friday demonstrates his bravery and humor by tricking a bear into a tree before killing it. Later, the group fights off a massive attack by hundreds of wolves.

They arrive safely in England. Crusoe settles his affairs, sells his plantation in Brazil, and marries. He has three children.

Final Narrative State

The book concludes with a brief summary of his later years. After his wife dies, he is persuaded by his nephew to go to sea again. He visits his island, finding that the Spaniards and the English mutineers have established a colony. He leaves them supplies and divides the land among them.

The narrator ends by stating that these later adventures, including battles with cannibals and travels to China, will be the subject of a future account, if the public desires it.

"All these things, with some very surprising incidents in some new adventures of my own, for ten years more, I may perhaps give a further account of hereafter."
